## easy

## medium

Many books about music theory are like medieval medical texts: full of strange symbols, old ideas, and explanations that are not clear or based on real reasons. This article tries to explain how harmony in music really works using a scientific approach. It looks at both the physical sounds in nature and the way the brain processes these sounds.

The discussion focuses on three key parts of music: the Major Scale, the common set of chords called the Standard Chord Dictionary, and the way Major and Minor triads give different feelings. While the Major Scale has been explained by earlier scientists like Helmholtz and Birkhoff, the explanations about the chord dictionary and why Major and Minor chords sound different are new and original.

The article points out a problem with Helmholtz's famous theory from the 19th century. He said that notes are in harmony because the combined sound is less bad than other combinations. But according to this idea, adding more notes should always make harmony worse, so the best harmony would be just a single note. This shows that his theory does not fully explain harmony.

The writing style is simple and friendly so that musicians and curious readers can understand the science behind tonal music. It also explains some wrong ideas that have confused people, like the myth of "undertones" and the use of the word "suspended" for certain chords, which comes from a false visual idea.

Overall, the article tries to be both a scientific contribution and an easy guide for anyone interested in how music creates harmony and feeling.

## hard

Most music theory books resemble medieval medical textbooks in their content: filled with unjustified superstitions, non-logical reasoning, and symbolic notations glorified by Latin phrases. This article seeks to present harmony, a key aspect of music, as a real scientific theory. The approach combines the physical phenomena of sound with the computational processes that any system, such as the human brain, must use to interpret sounds meaningfully.

The article derives three fundamental phenomena in music theory: the Major Scale, the Standard Chord Dictionary, and the distinct emotional qualities of Major and Minor Triads. Although previous scholars such as Helmholtz (1863) and Birkhoff (1933) independently derived the Major Scale, the explanatory derivation of the Standard Chord Dictionary and the explanation of the perceived emotional difference between Major and Minor Triads are regarded as original contributions.

Critically examining Helmholtz's 19th-century theory of harmony, the article finds it incomplete. Helmholtz argued that notes are in "concord" because the combined sound of those notes is "less worse" than other combinations. However, this implies that adding more notes can only degrade the harmony, albeit by lesser amounts in some cases. Thus, the most harmonious sound according to Helmholtz's theory would be a single note alone. This contradicts the observable existence of harmony as a musical phenomenon, exposing a fundamental flaw in that theory.

The article deliberately adopts a plain and conversational style to be accessible both to scientists interested in an original contribution to music science and to musicians or curious general readers frustrated by the lack of clear explanations about tonal music. It actively avoids unnecessarily formal language to facilitate understanding.

Additionally, it addresses common misconceptions, such as the mythical "undertones," which emerge from a misleading visual metaphor. It also clarifies terminologies, replacing "sustained" with "suspended" to describe a chord consisting of two interlocked fifths, while pointing out that the metaphor underlying the term "suspended" is itself a red herring.

In conclusion, this work bridges the gap between the physical principles of sound, computational processing of auditory information, and the artistic experience of harmony. It provides a fresh scientific perspective on established musical concepts, enriching both academic and practical understandings of music harmony.
