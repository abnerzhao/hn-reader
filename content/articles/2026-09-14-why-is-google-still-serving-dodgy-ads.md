## medium

Why is Google still serving dodgy ads?

A couple of months ago I saw an advert appear in the YouTube app that looked exactly like an iOS storage warning. I clicked it by accident while trying to free up space on my iPhone, and the experience felt like a genuine system alert. I reported the advert through the usual channel, expecting a quick review, but the reply I received was a generic note saying the ad did not violate Google’s policies and that sometimes things slip through the net.

I reported it a second time and got the identical response. In fact, several other users have filed the same complaint and received the same canned answer. Applying Hanlon’s razor, I prefer to blame oversight rather than deliberate malice, yet it is hard not to wonder whether Google benefits from leaving high‑click‑through, deceptive ads in place because they generate revenue.

A more straightforward explanation is that the human review team simply cannot examine every creative in detail, so misleading designs slip past. Given Google’s own AI models are capable of spotting such tricks, it seems reasonable to ask why they are not being deployed at scale. When I ran the suspect banner through Google’s Gemini, the model returned a clear DISAPPROVED verdict, citing multiple policy breaches.

Gemini flagged the ad for Misrepresentation: Misleading Ad Design because it mimics an iOS system alert modal with the text “iPhone Storage is Full” and uses iOS‑style typography and container styling. It also noted Non‑Functional / Deceptive UI Components, as the “Yes” and “No” buttons are static images that merely capture clicks to redirect to a landing page. Finally, the model identified Deceptive Fear‑Based Tactics & Unverified Claims, pointing to the warning that “If you don’t free up space soon, some features may not work properly,” which fabricates an urgent device‑failure scenario to provoke installs. Despite this instant AI rejection, the advert passed human review twice, showing a gap between Google’s detection capabilities and its enforcement.

Come on, meatbags – use some of the amazing AI tools you have access to.

## hard

A couple of months ago, I encountered an advert in the YouTube app that I only really registered after accidentally selecting it. I was conscious of having almost no storage left on my iPhone, so a brief lapse of attention was enough to make me fall for it. Even the most conscientious review systems occasionally allow these things through, which is why I performed my civic internet duty and reported the advert.

The response was disappointing. Google’s reply said, in effect, that some things sometimes escape scrutiny and that people do not always examine them properly. I reported it again and received the same answer. Several other people reportedly submitted complaints as well, yet each was met with essentially the same explanation: “We found that the ad doesn’t go against Google’s policies, which prohibit certain content and practices that we believe to be harmful to users and the overall online ecosystem.”

This invites Hanlon’s razor: never attribute to malice what can be explained by stupidity. Still, if one chooses to be less charitable, it becomes difficult not to wonder whether Google has a financial incentive to leave high-click adverts in circulation. Such ads are presumably attracting plenty of clicks, and advertisers do not pay for nothing. The money symbol says it all. A plainer explanation, however, is that human reviewers cannot scrutinise every advert properly, so some obviously suspect material slips through.

That is where AI ought to intervene. Google has access to sophisticated models; surely it can deploy them? Gemini, asked to assess the advert, produced an immediate and unmistakable rejection: “Classification: DISAPPROVED.” It identified two forms of misrepresentation—“Misleading Ad Design” and “Unreliable / Deceptive Claims”—and supplied detailed grounds for refusing the creative.

First, Gemini noted that Google Ads policy strictly prohibits adverts that imitate operating-system dialogs, system warnings, error messages, or interactive system notifications. The banner embedded in the creative reproduced the appearance of an iOS system alert labelled “iPhone Storage is Full,” complete with characteristic typography, container styling, and mock controls reading “Yes” and “No.” The point, Gemini concluded, was to deceive viewers into believing their device had generated a native system warning, thereby coercing them into clicking.

Second, it identified non-functional and deceptive UI components. The “Yes” and “No” options were static images rather than genuine controls. They did not perform the functions their labels implied. Instead, they created the illusion of a system choice while the entire ad banner redirected the user to a store or landing page. That is precisely the kind of false interactive element that advertising rules are intended to exclude.

Third, Gemini found deceptive fear-based tactics and unverified claims. The message “If you don’t free up space soon, some features may not work properly” appeared inside the counterfeit popup. In context, it fabricated an urgent state of system failure on the user’s own device. The advert therefore misrepresented the hardware’s condition in order to induce panic and drive app installs.

Gemini’s required action was equally categorical: disapprove the creative immediately; issue a policy-violation warning or disapproval flag to the advertiser account under Misrepresentation—Misleading Ad Design; and recognise that repeated offences of this kind may lead to full account suspension for deceptive practices.

The uncomfortable conclusion is that Google’s own model rejected the advert within seconds, while Google’s review process nevertheless approved it twice. AI had done in moments what the human process apparently failed to do. Come on, meatbags—use the amazing AI tools you already have.
