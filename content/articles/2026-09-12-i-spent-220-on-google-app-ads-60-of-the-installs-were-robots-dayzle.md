## easy

I run a small puzzle app. I spent $220 on Google ads. My goal was installs. At first nothing happened.

Then I saw many cheap installs. Sixty percent were not real users. These were robot accounts. The app opened once and closed fast. No one played any game. Google counted these as successful installs.

This made my ad cost go up. I changed my goal to winning a puzzle. Now I wait for a response. Bots can steal your budget. Real players matter more.

## medium

I run a small puzzle app called Dayzle. My background in numbers makes marketing optimization very interesting for me. Two weeks ago I started a Google Ads campaign for Android with a daily budget of CA$40, aiming for installs. At first the campaign spent almost nothing. I had set a target cost per install of $1.50, but Google could not find any installs at that price. As a test I removed the target, and the campaign immediately spent double my daily budget, CA$80, reporting 21 installs. I felt excited. However, checking my admin panel revealed only one install. The system had missed most of them because older versions of the app do not report an install date. In the raw analytics there were 21 new Android devices that day, and 20 of them ran an outdated version of the app that the Play Store had stopped distributing days earlier. Those phones could not have gotten the app from the Play Store, so they must have downloaded it from another source, even though every device claimed Google Play was the installer. Each opened the app once, spent zero seconds on any screen, and never returned. There were 28 different phone models across 19 states, showing remarkable consistency among these twenty identical behaviors.

Over the entire two weeks the billing showed 56 installs, including 33 with the suspicious pattern, seven from countries outside the targeted region, and thirteen genuine users. The thirteen real users completed 92 puzzles together, which suggests they genuinely enjoyed the product. The thirty-three questionable installs appeared to behave like bots rather than real people. The analytics export confirms this suspicion. Google optimizes based on the goal provided, and here the goal was simply installs. This farm would watch the shortest video in the ad group without clicking it, then install the app from a saved copy instead of from the store, since that process was faster and less likely to trigger detection by Google Play. Google counts a view followed by an install as a successful conversion, creating a paradox where the more the farm installed the app, the better it looked to Google's algorithm. Consequently, Google sent more ads to this farm, which increased its installation rate further. A cycle emerged that ensured ad spending was wasted.

Currently I am waiting for Google's response to the invalid-traffic form. The original campaign goal has been changed to won a puzzle instead of opened the app. Making a simple script open an app and click around is easier than solving a Sudoku puzzle. The strategy aims to make the application more costly to farm than competing apps. Larger applications require more effort to automate, offering decent protection against such problems. I plan to report on the refund once the investigation concludes. This serves as a warning: if someone relies solely on Google's install count for advertising performance, they should examine their data thoroughly. Even a modest bot farm can drain a small ad budget if it finds a way through.

## hard

I run a small puzzle app called Dayzle, and I’m a numbers guy, so a marketing optimization problem is pretty much right up my alley. Two weeks ago, I switched on a Google Ads campaign for Android at CA$40 a day, with installs set as the goal. For the first few days, it barely spent anything. My target cost per install was $1.50, and Google apparently couldn’t find installs at that price. As a test, I removed the target, and the campaign immediately spent twice my daily budget, CA$80, while reporting 21 installs. I was excited for about as long as it took to check the admin panel, which said the number was 1.

I didn’t need to be a numbers guy to see that something didn’t add up. The reason the panel had missed most of them was that old versions of the app don’t report an install date. When I went into the raw analytics, there were 21 new Android devices that day, and 20 of them were running an old version of the app that the Play Store had stopped serving days earlier. You can’t get that old version from Play, so those phones must have got the app somewhere else, even though every one of them claimed Google Play was the installer.

Their behaviour was even less convincing. Each one opened the app once, spent zero seconds on any screen, and never came back. That meant 28 phone models across 19 states, which is a lot of variety for 20 phones that all did exactly the same thing. Over the whole two weeks, the total was 56 installs billed: 33 with that same pattern, 7 more from countries the campaign wasn’t targeting, and 13 people. The 13 people finished 92 games between them, which is a nice signal that real people enjoyed what we’ve built.

The 33 weren’t behaving like people, so I suspected a bot farm, and the analytics export bears that out. Google optimizes for whatever goal you give it, and my goal was installs. This farm would watch the shortest video in my ad group without clicking it, then install our app from a saved copy of the file instead of from the store, because that’s faster and Play might notice. Google counts a view followed by an install as a conversion, so the irony is that the more the farm “installed” our app, the better it looked to Google’s algorithm.

That created a loop that guaranteed my ad spend was wasted. The farm installed the app, Google read those installs as success, and Google sent more of my ads to the farm, which installed it more. Where I am now is waiting on Google’s answer to the invalid-traffic form, and the campaign’s goal is now “won a puzzle” instead of “opened the app.” It’s low effort to make a script open an app and click around; it’s higher effort to make one solve a Sudoku.

The idea is simply to make us more expensive to farm than the next app. That’s probably decent protection for an app my size. Larger apps are worth the extra effort, and I’d guess they see a lot more of this than they know. I’ll report back on the refund. So I guess this is my PSA: if you’re relying on Google’s install count for your ads, it’s a real number, but it’s definitely worth digging into.

If a bot farm can find my tiny ad budget, it can definitely find yours.
