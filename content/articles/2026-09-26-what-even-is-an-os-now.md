## easy

AI is changing how we use computers. Now, people can make their own apps by just talking to the computer. They solve small, personal problems. Soon, most software will be built for one or two people.

This changes what an operating system does. Old systems separate apps from strangers. But now, users will create their own software. The system will need to be flexible and help them build things.

A new phone is being built for this future. It will not run pre-made apps like current phones. Instead, you can ask it to build an app for you, on the phone, whenever you need it.

The goal is to return to a time when computers seemed magical.

## medium

Crazy as it sounds, the real impact of computing's AI revolution hasn't fully sunk in yet. I'm starting fresh on a new project with Kurt, so I should mention I'm talking about my own work. It reminds me of getting our first computer when I was a kid—a basic Z80 machine that booted straight into BASIC. I was too young to learn programming, and that early experience taught me that computers weren't as amazing as I'd imagined. Later, security work pulled me into coding, which only made building complex applications seem even more daunting. I could write network servers easily, but a word processor felt impossible.

Suddenly, everything has changed. Computers now work the way I dreamed they would as a child. AI has erased the boundaries between backend and frontend programming, between web and native applications. But even more importantly, it's breaking down the wall between programmers and users. My computer is now full of simple programs I created myself, using English sentences instead of code. This shift isn't just for tech experts—it's inevitable for everyone.

I think we're so focused on AI's immediate effects that we're missing the bigger picture. What happens when most software is created for just one or two people? We're moving away from buying pre-made applications from strangers. Instead, users will generate their own solutions for very specific needs—like getting hyperlocal weather forecasts or finding meeting times. These seem like small problems, but they're too niche for traditional software companies to address.

Traditional operating systems were designed to manage applications from unknown developers. But that structure makes less sense when most software comes from you or people you know. It's even less relevant when applications can constantly evolve based on user needs. This is exactly what we're building: a phone that isn't designed for fixed-function apps. It creates software on-demand, in English, whenever you need it.

I know this sounds like typical startup hype about the future. The tech world has felt like 100 years of change compressed into one lately. AI keeps breaking through limitations we thought were permanent. When we were kids, we believed computers could do anything if we could just access them. We learned as professionals just how limited they were. Now we all need to unlearn that and think like children again—because that's what AI enables. So here we go.

## hard

The impact of artificial intelligence on computing has taken longer than a single year to fully register, yet the transformation itself remains largely invisible to most observers. As the individual matured through adolescence, computer security gradually introduced programming into their routine practice, revealing the daunting complexity behind seemingly simple applications. The realization that constructing even basic word processing required understanding intricate underlying architectures fundamentally altered their perspective on technological development. While the ability to deploy high-performance network servers remained straightforward, the challenge of creating specialized document editors appeared insurmountable—a metaphorical comparison to building a diesel locomotive rather than a simple server. This progression culminated in a recognition that contemporary computing operates under entirely different principles than those initially envisioned during formative years.

The narrative then pivots toward examining how artificial intelligence is systematically eroding traditional boundaries between computational layers. Modern applications increasingly blur distinctions between backend logic and frontend presentation, merging web technologies with native platforms in ways that defy conventional architectural separation. The author observes that everyday interface elements—menu bars and task switches—now contain icons representing programs conceived personally, using natural language as the primary programming medium. This democratization of creation suggests an imminent convergence where users themselves become architects of their digital environments.

This shift challenges established assumptions about software distribution and execution models. Historically, applications emerged from commercial vendors selling finished products, but emerging patterns indicate a transition toward collaborative ecosystems where individuals construct tailored solutions from shared building blocks. Traditional operating systems, which traditionally served merely as resource allocation mechanisms, actually function primarily as isolation frameworks that separate competing applications and regulate their interactions. In contexts where software originates from diverse external sources, maintaining strict compartmentalization becomes essential for stability and security.

The proposed vision represents a radical departure from current paradigms: a platform designed to enable continuous personal app generation directly on mobile devices. Rather than relying on predetermined applications manufactured by professional developers, this approach empowers users to dynamically assemble functionality suited to specific needs. The philosophy rejects the notion of universal, fixed-purpose software in favor of fluid, context-sensitive experiences that emerge from direct interaction between human intent and intelligent assistance.

Critically, the author acknowledges potential skepticism regarding these predictions, noting that similar grand visions often fail to materialize upon public announcement. However, the historical trajectory of technological disruption suggests that the current period may indeed represent a transformative epoch comparable to previous generational shifts. The rapid pace of innovation, characterized by weekly advancements in model capabilities, implies that previously unimaginable functionalities will become accessible within months. Ultimately, the message advocates for abandoning the assumption that computers require specialized expertise to operate, encouraging instead a return to intuitive, childlike approaches to technological engagement.

The analysis concludes by emphasizing the necessity of reimagining foundational computing infrastructure. Traditional operating systems, optimized for distributing standardized applications across homogeneous environments, prove inadequate for scenarios involving heterogeneous, continuously evolving software ecosystems. As applications increasingly share common components and mutate organically, the rigid architecture of legacy systems becomes an obstacle rather than an enabler. The proposed solution prioritizes adaptability and interoperability, positioning the platform as a dynamic environment where users can construct, modify, and deploy personalized computational tools with minimal friction.
