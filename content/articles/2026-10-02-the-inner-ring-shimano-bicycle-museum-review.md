## easy

The Shimano Bicycle Museum is in Sakai, near Osaka. Its building is made of glass and steel.

Inside, dark rooms hold many old bikes under lights. One room shows a famous steel bike called Flandria. You can see its parts clearly.

Visitors can touch some bikes and feel different metal weights. A special room holds a rare Olympic bike. They are open for everyone to explore.

The museum focuses on bicycles, not on Shimano business. It is a nice place to learn about cycles. Many people visit and leave happy.

## medium

A look at the Shimano Bicycle Museum, with praise for its focus on bicycles over its own corporate history. For anyone in the Kansai area of Japan, perhaps visiting Kyoto or Osaka, this is a prodigious collection of machines within a well-designed setting. The museum itself is striking, a modern building of glass and steel. To see pictures of it online is to imagine it might be Shimano’s head office in a prime location, and that there’s a museum inside. However, it is all museum, and the surrounding area is more ordinary. A welcome message on the wall at the start of the tour reads, “In Japanese, we call a bicycle ‘jitensha,’ which literally means ‘wheels rotated by oneself.’” This means a bicycle is operated by the rider and that there are as many ways of enjoying cycling as there are riders. You might learn the word for bicycle here as you enter; by the time you leave, the message of many ways to enjoy cycling will have come through. The sign is of course in Japanese too, and the museum is bilingual.

Head upstairs and you enter a large, darkened room with a procession of bicycles under spotlights. These cover the chronology of the machine, from rudimentary hobby horses to the arrival of pedals, the chain, and tires—more evolution. This large space also doubles as an auditorium, and an educational film is played at times that covers the history of the bicycle and the origins of cycle sport, all within a wider societal context that touches on war, industrialisation, and consumerism. Then it’s into a bright white room where frosted glass lets in daylight, and more spotlights highlight exhibits such as Freddy Maerten’s steel Flandria. You can get close to touch it—not that you would or should—but it’s not behind glass or roped off, just raised on a small stage. You can see the Ishiwata tubing decal and the Shimano Crane rear mech, the beginnings of Shimano in the pro peloton. All the other bikes are similarly accessible. You can see bikes used at Paralympic games and notice the custom modifications up close. A Specialized Stumpjumper, one of the first mountain bikes to go on sale, sits there, and you can spot a Campagnolo headset and motorbike brake levers among the components on the “clunker.”

As well as history, there’s the humdrum. A Crescent bike in the yellow and blue of the Swedish postal service, complete with studded tires to show you can ride on ice, even with a heavy cargo. A very much used Cannondale is loaded with luggage, spare tires wrapped around the bags like boa constrictors, and all caked in dust, as if Tatsu Sakimoto had come in and parked it mid-way during his four-year, 55,000km journey around the world. A Merida bike from the 2021 Olympics road race is there, complete with scratches galore and crash dents. The event feels like yesterday, but the bike already looks ancient. Plenty of kids’ bikes feature too, shopping bikes and more. Everyone ought to recognise something they’ve ridden. Stands explore materials science with a comparison of steel, aluminium, and titanium tubing, and the chance to feel the weight of these metals yourself. Another lets you pick up a racing bike to feel how light it is. But among all this educational material is the simple message that bikes can do plenty. It’s actually hard to find much about Shimano and its origins here. There’s a mention that gun blacksmiths in Sakai started repairing imported bikes in the late 19th century and soon began manufacture. For what it’s worth, the city Sakai has long been a big steel production area, first for swords and then into other goods including guns and cutlery. It’s here that Shimano started too, but it’s only later and in passing that you come across a small item that was Shimano’s first product: a freewheel. Shozaburo Shimano founded the company to make these, and one thing led to another. Including fishing freewheels and gearing can be applied inside reels, and today one quarter of Shimano’s revenue, about a billion Euros, comes from fishing—but there’s not a rod nor reel in sight.

Still, the museum is visibly the Shimano museum in other ways. There’s an excellent stand where a racing bike has been broken into pieces; apparently, there are 3,200 parts in one bike if you really count every chain pin, bolt, and electronic circuit component. Of course, it’s a Shimano Dura-Ace groupset in the display. Good luck finding SRAM or Campagnolo, but there are a few examples, even a Mavic rear mech. The top floor of the museum includes also a set of display cases for the flagship Dura-Ace and XTR groups, starting with the 1973 version, then 1984, 1996, 2004, 2012, 2016, 2021, complete with four empty displays waiting to house 2027 and the rest of what the future brings. There’s also a library, and behind this, a locked archive for researchers. This is valuable; the museum might house one of each type of bike, but here the shelves are full of books, brochures, and catalogues—a treasure of primary source material. This section marks the end of the tour, but you’re free to wander rather than be guided to a gift store and the exit.

The verdict: an enjoyable celebration of the bicycle and a good visual history of the evolution of machines. This might be the Shimano Bicycle Museum, and it is full of Shimano componentry, but this is firmly a bicycle museum with very little about corporate history and no hard sell either. If you want a Shimano t-shirt or some components, you won’t find them here. It covers the history of bicycles and puts the machine first, all sorts sit under spotlights and pleasantly within reach if you want to look at any details up close, from the crinkled cast iron of early bikes to the exploded disassembly of a modern race bike down to the last component. There are other bicycle museums in the world, but many are smaller and have the feel of hobby projects. Sometimes there can be excellent temporary collections in science or art museums—Merckx-Ickx was a fun example. This one works well as a permanent collection—Giant has a possibly similar one in Taiwan—and is enhanced by its architecture. It is spacious (4,223m² says the museum spec) and peaceful inside. The top floor with its research archive bolsters all of this. Access: Sakai is a city about 15km south of Osaka, Japan. It’s a 30-minute train ride from Osaka central station to Sakai or Sakaihigashi stations; the latter is closer to the museum. There’s bike parking, of course. Admission is listed JPY 500 (less than €3), but has the price gone up? I didn’t keep the receipt, but a vague memory of having paid more. It’s still cheap if they doubled the price.

## hard

The Shimano Bicycle Museum stands in Sakai, approximately fifteen kilometers south of Osaka, Japan, offering visitors a striking modern structure of glass and steel that houses a comprehensive collection of bicycles while deliberately keeping corporate history in the background. The building itself evokes the impression of Shimano's headquarters in a prime urban location, yet the exterior suggests primarily a museum rather than a commercial facility. Upon entering, visitors encounter a welcome message in Japanese stating that a bicycle, known locally as jitensha, translates literally to 'wheels rotated by oneself,' emphasizing that cycling offers countless modes of enjoyment depending on the rider. This concept becomes increasingly clear throughout the tour as the narrative of many ways to experience cycling unfolds through the curated displays.

High above the main galleries, a large darkened chamber reveals a procession of bicycles illuminated by spotlights, arranged chronologically from primitive hobby horses through the introduction of pedals, chains, tires, and subsequent technological evolutions. This expansive space serves dual purposes as both an exhibition hall and an auditorium, where educational films periodically illuminate the broader historical context connecting bicycles to war, industrialization, and consumer culture. Transitioning upward leads to a bright white room bathed in natural daylight filtered through frosted glass, where specific exhibits draw attention to Shimano's pioneering contributions. Among these is Freddy Maerten's steel Flandria, displayed on a small raised platform without protective barriers, allowing viewers to observe details such as the Ishiwata tubing decal and the Shimano Crane rear mechanism that mark the origins of Shimano's presence in professional cycling.

The museum extends beyond specialized machinery to showcase everyday bicycles that reflect global usage patterns. A Crescent bicycle in the distinctive yellow and blue colors of Sweden's postal service demonstrates capability on icy surfaces thanks to studded tires designed for carrying heavy cargo. A particularly worn Cannondale features luggage strapped around its frame like boa constrictors and appears thoroughly caked in dust, suggesting the journey of Tatsu Sakimoto who rode it for four years covering fifty-five thousand kilometers. A Merida bicycle from the 2021 Olympic road race bears numerous scratches and crash dents, appearing almost ancient despite its recent competition appearance. Additionally, various children's bicycles and shopping models provide recognition for those who have once ridden them themselves.

Educational installations allow visitors to engage physically with materials science, enabling tactile exploration of steel, aluminum, and titanium tubing through direct handling. One interactive exhibit permits participants to lift a racing bike and experience its remarkable lightness. Beyond these hands-on experiences, the museum maintains scholarly resources including a dedicated library and a locked archive reserved for researchers seeking primary source documents. The top floor houses extensive display cases showcasing the flagship Dura-Ace and XTR groups beginning with their 1973 prototype, followed by versions from 1984, 1996, 2004, 2012, 2016, 2021, and currently awaiting the next iteration in 2027. These chronological displays demonstrate the continuous evolution of competitive cycling technology over nearly half a century.

Historical context situates the museum within Sakai's rich industrial heritage, noting that the city developed as a major steel production center initially for swords before expanding into weapons, cutlery, and eventually bicycle manufacturing. Gun blacksmiths in Sakai established repair shops for imported bicycles in the late nineteenth century, and shortly thereafter began producing their own equipment. Shozaburo Shimano founded the company specifically to manufacture freewheels, an innovation that ultimately expanded into diverse applications including fishing gear. Today, approximately one quarter of Shimano's annual revenue derives from fishing tackle—roughly one billion euros—demonstrating how a single invention spawned an entire industry. Despite this commercial success, the museum presents minimal information about the company's origins, maintaining instead a focus squarely on the bicycle as the subject of its exhibitions.

The overall assessment positions the Shimano Bicycle Museum as a distinguished institution that celebrates the bicycle as the primary theme rather than serving as a vehicle for corporate promotion. While the collection prominently features Shimano components, the presentation prioritizes the machine itself, placing human experience and mechanical development at the forefront. Visitors may choose between following a structured tour or exploring freely, with the option to browse the gift shop upon exiting. Located conveniently near Osaka central station via a thirty-minute train journey to Sakaihigashi station, the museum remains affordable at JPY 500 admission, providing accessible entry to this comprehensive visual history of bicycle evolution.
