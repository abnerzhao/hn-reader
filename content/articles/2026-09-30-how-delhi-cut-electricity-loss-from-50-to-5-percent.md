## easy

One cold January morning in 2002, power went out again. My family lost light, heat, and food. Children cried because they could not use the microwave.

Delhi fixed its power lines and stopped stealing electricity. Loss fell from fifty percent to five percent by 2026. Now the city runs smoothly and stays safe. New meters gave exact bills and stopped cheating.

Streetlights shine bright and fans run without interruption. Businesses grow and people feel more confident.

Other cities can copy Delhi’s plan. Problems like old wires and theft can be solved.

## medium

It’s 6 a.m. on a cold January morning in 2002 in New Delhi. It’s still dark outside, and I’m in the kitchen preparing breakfast, packing lunches, and getting my two children ready to catch the school bus when, for the third time in a week, the power goes out. No lights, no mixer to finish my daughter’s puttu—her favorite rice dish—no kettle, no toaster. The bathroom is dark, and the kids are upset. It will probably be hours before the power comes back on, so I grab a flashlight and light the candles that are set up around the house for these occasions. We’re behind schedule now. We pack the food we have, bundle up as the house turns chilly, and head outside, leaving a mess in the kitchen. We make our way to the bus stop in the dark—the streetlights are out, too—only to discover my daughter has missed her ride. Again. I’ll be late for work at Jamia Millia Islamia, a university where I am a professor of electrical engineering and teach power systems and smart grids. I just hope the power is on there.

This was a common scene for my family and all of Delhi in the early 2000s. Power outages happened almost daily and lasted hours. When the power was on, the quality was so poor that it would dim lights, flicker screens, and wreak havoc on appliances. Customer service at the power utilities essentially didn’t exist. These problems had been getting worse through the 1980s and 1990s. The cause: an aging distribution grid bereft of crucial technologies, and electricity providers with little accountability. The situation became so bad that the city was losing more than half of its power through obsolete equipment and theft. These staggering losses meant that utilities got paid for only a fraction of the electricity they were trying to deliver. And the lack of funds prevented them from investing in better grid infrastructure.

But over the last quarter century, a remarkable effort by the government and the city’s distribution utilities has turned Delhi’s grid into a reliable, modern system. Power losses have shrunk from over 50 percent in 2002 to 5 to 6 percent in 2026—on par with France and Belgium, and better than Greece and Serbia. Delhi’s grid reliability index, a measure of how often electricity can be counted on, stood at around 70 percent in 2002 and has now topped 99.9 percent. With reliable power, businesses across the city have blossomed. The streetlights are bright. The number of electric vehicles, including city buses, is growing daily. Quality of life has improved. Today, my family is comfortable year-round in our home despite Delhi’s scorching summers and cold winters. The chaos of losing power no longer hinders me from getting to work. The city still has problems—pollution, overcrowding, noise—but thankfully, reliable power is no longer among them.

The transformation of Delhi’s grid can serve as a model for other cities that suffer from decrepit power infrastructure. By the early 2000s, Delhi’s nearly 100-year-old power distribution system was in serious disrepair. Everything was old—lines, transformers, circuit breakers, switches. The shabby state of the grid caused high electricity losses, where electricity vanishes primarily as heat. The cause was a classic electrical problem: too much current flowing through a network that wasn’t designed to carry it efficiently. Reactive power, necessary for many devices, caused the voltage along the line to drop. When the voltage fell, devices tried to maintain performance by drawing more current, which produced even greater losses in a vicious cycle. In addition to these technical problems, theft of electricity was rampant, and customers didn’t pay their bills. Meters were old and easily tampered with. The combined commercial and technical losses left Delhi’s utilities collecting payment for less than half of the electricity they were supplying in the early 2000s.

In 2002, Delhi was already taking drastic action to fix its grid. The organization overseeing Delhi’s distribution, the Delhi Vidyut Board, was broken up and two private companies—BSES (now Reliance Infrastructure), and Tata Power—took over distribution. They faced a Herculean task, inheriting a deteriorated and overloaded network, massive power theft, weak billing and collection systems, and an aging, largely untrained workforce. The utilities revamped their organizational structures, installed digital SCADA systems for real-time grid monitoring, and replaced aging equipment. Crucially, they installed hundreds of capacitor banks to supply reactive power and stabilize voltage. To reduce theft, they replaced bare distribution wires with insulated lines. They also replaced old electromechanical meters with digital ones and created web-based payment systems. In areas where theft was particularly rampant, the companies worked to improve water supply and provide educational opportunities, paying women to collect electricity payments from their neighbors. Bill payment rates from these areas are now on par with the rest of the city.

The results have been transformative. The bustling Main Bazar in the Paharganj neighborhood of Delhi increasingly uses more nighttime electricity, but reductions in electricity loss help counter demand. The transformation of Delhi’s grid demonstrates that it is possible to improve electricity in places with similar problems by adapting the changes Delhi made. Regions of Albania, Argentina, Bangladesh, Brazil, and others are reeling from heavy losses in their distribution grids, and their problems look like Delhi’s 25 years ago. The journey involved all stakeholders, including customers, city authorities, and utility employees at all levels, and it offers a viable path forward.

## hard

It is 6 a.m. on a cold January morning in 2002 in New Delhi. The house is still dark, and I am in the kitchen preparing breakfast, packing lunches and getting my two children ready for the school bus when, for the third time that week, the power goes out. No lights, no mixer to finish my daughter’s puttu—her favourite rice dish—no kettle, no toaster. The bathroom is dark and the children are upset. I know it will probably be hours before the supply returns, so I seize a flashlight and light the candles that are kept for such occasions. We fall behind schedule, bundle up as the house grows chilly and head outside, leaving a mess behind in the kitchen. At the bus stop, street‑lamps are also out; we discover that my daughter has missed her ride again. I will be late for work at Jamia Millia Islamia, where I teach electrical engineering and specialise in power systems and smart grids, and I can only hope that the campus still has electricity.

That scene was typical for my family and for most of Delhi in the early 2000s. Outages occurred almost daily and lasted for hours; when the current was on, the voltage was so poor that lights dimmed, screens flickered and appliances behaved erratically. Customer service at the utilities hardly existed. By July 2007 a store in New Delhi specialising in reconditioned generators did a brisk trade, driven by the fear of summer heat‑induced cuts. The problems had been worsening through the 1980s and 1990s. The distribution network was ageing, lacked essential technologies and the utilities faced little accountability. Consequently more than half of the electricity generated was lost through obsolete equipment and theft, leaving the companies paid for only a fraction of what they tried to deliver. The shortage of revenue prevented any meaningful investment in a better grid.

Over the following twenty‑five years a concerted effort by the government and Delhi’s distribution utilities transformed the system into a reliable, modern network. Technical losses fell from above 50 percent in 2002 to between 5 and 6 percent in 2026—a level comparable with France and Belgium and better than Greece or Serbia. The grid reliability index, which measures how often electricity can be counted on, rose from roughly 70 percent in 2002 to above 99.9 percent today. Streetlights now blaze steadily, the number of electric vehicles—including city buses—grows each day, and the overall quality of life has improved. My family is comfortable year‑round despite Delhi’s scorching summers and bitter winters; the chaos of losing power no longer hinders my commute. The city still wrestles with pollution, overcrowding and noise, but dependable electricity is no longer among its problems.

Delhi hosts the national capital and lies on the Yamuna River in the north of the country. About 23 million people live here, making it one of the most densely populated agglomerations on earth. The grid comprises thousands of kilometres of lines; peak demand reached an all‑time high of 8 748 megawatts this year. Approximately 76 percent of the electricity is bought from central generating companies and private producers in neighbouring states, while generation within the city is limited to natural gas and renewable sources. The present mix is roughly 48.5 percent coal, 26.5 percent natural gas and the remainder carbon‑free, led by hydropower at 15.6 percent. Tata Power‑DDL, for instance, replaced about five kilometres of overhead conductors with underground cables in the Janta Flats area of Shalimar Bagh, cutting losses and improving the streetscape.

By the early 2000s Delhi’s almost century‑old distribution system was in serious disrepair. Conductors, transformers, circuit breakers and switches were all outdated, and there was scarcely any money to modernise them. The poor state of the network produced high electricity losses, which disappear mainly as heat. To understand why, recall that a typical power system consists of generation, transmission and distribution. After generation, transformers raise the voltage to high levels—132, 220, 400 or 765 kilovolts in India—for transmission over long distances. At receiving substations the voltage is stepped down (to 66, 33 or 11 kV) and distribution lines carry the power to consumers. The system operates principally on alternating current. Distribution lines convey both active power—the real energy that does useful work, measured in watts—and reactive power, which shuttles back and forth, building electric and magnetic fields (measured in volt‑ampere‑reactive, VAR). Although reactive power does not perform work, it is essential for devices with inductance or capacitance, such as motors, transformers and computers. When many reactive loads share a line, the total current (active + reactive) must increase. Greater current raises I²R losses, heats the conductors and lowers the voltage. Reduced voltage causes appliances to draw still more current to maintain performance, which further increases losses and depresses voltage—a vicious cycle. In parts of Delhi the effect was so severe that residents installed their own voltage stabilisers; at my home we bought an inverter‑battery set to keep a fan and a few lights alive during the frequent outages.

Losses in Delhi were not purely technical. Electricity theft was rampant, practiced by the powerful and the powerless alike. Businesses, households and even utility employees with vested interests would siphon power by tapping into streetlights or nearby distribution lines. The utilities lacked the means to detect or punish such behaviour, and the courts were already overburdened; an effective electricity regulator had not yet been formed. Billing was equally problematic. Old electromechanical meters were often faulty and easy to tamper with. Meter readers visited premises, noted the figure in a ledger, entered it later at the office and produced a bill that was left at the customer’s door—a process rife with error. Consumers had to queue for hours at utility offices, which kept limited hours; many simply avoided paying because there was no penalty for non‑payment and no threat of disconnection. (I sometimes asked a family member to stand in line for me.) The combination of commercial and technical losses meant that the utilities collected payment for less than half of the electricity they supplied in the early 2000s.

India’s systemic shortcomings were not confined to Delhi. In 2002 state utilities nationwide averaged losses of nearly 37 percent, and the fragmented division of authority between central and state governments hampered reform. The 2001‑drafted Electricity Act, 2003 eventually unbundled generation, transmission and distribution, opened the sector to privatisation, allowed large consumers to bypass local distributors, created a central regulator for interstate tariffs and market competition, and introduced mechanisms for prosecuting theft. In 2002 Delhi already began decisive action: the Delhi Vidyut Board was split, and two private firms—BSES (now Reliance Infrastructure) and Tata Power—took over distribution. Tata Power, serving the northern half, inherited a combined commercial and technical loss of 53.5 percent; BSES faced 51.5 percent losses in South Delhi and a striking 63.1 percent in East Delhi. As Tata’s CEO remarked, the utilities received a deteriorated, overloaded network, massive theft, weak billing and collection, faulty consumer records and an largely untrained workforce, together with over 100 000 unresolved billing complaints, 20 000 pending connection applications and frequent supply failures that had eroded trust. Both companies instituted organisational overhauls, reduced the influence of junior staff, created specialised teams and gave long‑term employees retraining from the newcomers. Technically they deployed SCADA (supervisory control and data acquisition) systems for real‑time monitoring, replaced ageing transformers and breakers, instituted rigorous maintenance schedules and installed hundreds of capacitor banks—some mobile—to supply reactive power at strategic points, thereby lowering line current and stabilising voltage. Voltage regulators were placed where drops tended to occur. To deter theft they swapped bare conductors for insulated three‑phase cables, which also reduced ground‑fault‑related outages. Workers received helmet‑mounted voltage sensors and thermal scanners for safe, accurate work. Old meters were swapped for digital units read by handheld devices; in some areas radio‑frequency group metering consolidated several customers’ readings into a single wireless transmission to a central database, eliminating individual reads. Smart meters are now being piloted to give consumers bill control and utilities remote equipment operation (with consent). Payment was facilitated by 24‑hour kiosks, a web portal and a mobile app, alongside incentives for early settlement and community‑engagement programmes. In slum areas where losses reached as high as 83 percent, Tata Power and later BSES employed local women—known as abhas—to knock on doors, remind neighbours to pay and even organised literacy and financial‑training programmes for them. Payment collection in those neighbourhoods now matches the citywide average, and the utility’s aggregate losses have settled at the 5‑to‑6‑percent range demonstrated today.
