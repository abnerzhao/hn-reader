## easy

Many books promise quick lessons in a programming language. They only show a little syntax. They do not teach how to think or solve real problems. Real skill does not come that fast.

Studies show it takes about ten years of work to get good. You must try tasks a bit harder than you can do. See what went wrong, fix it, then do it again and again.

Program because you enjoy it. Work with other programmers, read their code, and talk about it. Learn a few languages and know how a computer runs steps. This kind of practice builds real ability over time.

## medium

Walk into any bookstore and you will see endless variations of books promising to teach you Java, C, or SQL in just a few hours or days. A search for titles claiming to teach a subject in a few hours reveals hundreds of results, most of which are programming books. This suggests either that people are in an immense rush to learn, or that programming is somehow easier to master than any other skill. However, a title like 'Teach Yourself C++ in 24 Hours' is misleading. In a single day, you cannot write significant programs or truly understand a complex programming environment; you can only achieve a superficial familiarity with syntax, which is a dangerous substitute for deep understanding.

True expertise in any field—be it chess, music composition, or neuropsychology—typically takes about ten years of deliberative practice. This is not simply about repeating a task, but about constantly challenging yourself with tasks just beyond your current ability, analyzing your performance, and correcting mistakes. Even geniuses like Mozart required years of intense work before producing world-class music. While some suggest a benchmark of 10,000 hours to reach the highest levels of performance, the reality is that expertise is a long-term commitment of effort, repetition, and constant refinement.

To become a successful programmer, you must start with interest and find enough joy in the process to sustain a decade of effort. The most effective learning happens by doing. While formal education can provide deep theoretical insights and professional credentials, book learning alone is insufficient. Real skill is developed by interacting with other programmers, reading existing code, and working on projects. It is helpful to be the best programmer on some projects to learn leadership and vision, and the worst on others to learn from the masters and understand how to maintain complex systems.

A well-rounded programmer should master several different types of languages: one emphasizing class abstractions, one functional, one syntactic, one declarative, and one focused on parallelism. You should also understand the hardware, knowing the relative speeds of CPU instructions versus disk seeks. Ultimately, while books are useful starting points, real expertise is forged through years of hands-on experience. It is a long craft to learn, and while anyone can start, it takes a fearless commitment to excellence to truly master it.

## hard

Walk into any bookstore and you will see shelves stacked with titles promising to teach yourself Java in 24 hours, C in a weekend, SQL in a day, Ruby in a few hours, and countless other languages in similarly short spans. An Amazon advanced search for books with the words teach, yourself, and hours since 2000 returns over five hundred matches, and nine of the top ten are programming manuals (the tenth concerns bookkeeping). Replacing “teach yourself” with “learn” or “hours” with “days” yields a comparable pattern. The implication is either that people are in a tremendous hurry to acquire programming knowledge, or that programming is somehow far easier to master than any other discipline. This trend is noted even in academic circles; for example, the authors of How to Design Programs observe that bad programming can be picked up by novices in just three weeks, while the Abtruse Goose comic offers a satirical take on the same phenomenon.

Consider what a title like Teach Yourself C++ in 24 Hours actually promises. In a single day there is simply no time to write several substantial programs, to reflect on successes and failures, or to sit beside an experienced developer and absorb the feel of working in a C++ environment. Consequently, such a book can only convey a superficial familiarity with the language, not a deep understanding. As Alexander Pope warned, a little learning is a dangerous thing. Within twenty‑four hours you might memorize some of C++’s syntax—assuming you already know another language—but you would not grasp how to employ the language effectively. A BASIC programmer, for instance, could learn to write BASIC‑style programs using C++ syntax, yet would remain ignorant of what C++ is truly good for and where it falls short. Alan Perlis put it bluntly: a language that does not change the way you think about programming is not worth knowing. One conceivable motive for such a quick study is to acquire just enough of C++ (or more likely JavaScript or Processing) to interface with an existing tool for a specific task; in that case you are learning to accomplish the task, not to program.

Research on expertise shows that true mastery in diverse fields—chess, music composition, telegraph operation, painting, piano, swimming, tennis, neuropsychology, topology—typically requires about ten years of deliberate practice. This means engaging with tasks that lie just beyond your current ability, attempting them, analysing performance during and after the effort, correcting mistakes, and repeating the cycle. There appear to be no genuine shortcuts; even Mozart, who demonstrated prodigious talent at age four, needed another thirteen years before producing music of world‑class stature. The Beatles burst onto the scene with a string of number‑one hits and an appearance on the Ed Sullivan Show in 1964, but they had been honing their craft in Liverpool and Hamburg clubs since 1957, and their first critically acclaimed album, Sgt. Pepper’s, did not arrive until 1967. Henri Cartier‑Bresson summed up the idea with his remark that one’s first ten thousand photographs are the worst, a observation that digital cameras have made easier to reach in a week. Samuel Johnson declared that excellence in any field can be won only by the labour of a lifetime, while Chaucer lamented that life is short but the craft long to learn. The ancient Hippocratic aphorism “ars longa, vita brevis” expands to “Life is short, the craft long, opportunity fleeting, experiment treacherous, judgment difficult.” As K. Anders Ericsson observes, even the most gifted individuals need years of ten to twenty hours per week to reach the highest levels of performance; the oft‑cited ten‑thousand‑hour rule merely gives a sense of the scale involved.

If you aspire to become a programmer, the recipe is straightforward: cultivate genuine interest, program because it is fun, and ensure that enjoyment persists so you are willing to invest the necessary ten years or ten thousand hours. The most effective learning occurs by doing; performance does not rise automatically with mere experience, but improves when seasoned practitioners deliberately strive to get better. Effective learning demands a well‑defined task pitched at the right difficulty, informative feedback, and ample opportunity for repetition and correction of errors. These principles are echoed in Cognition in Practice: Mind, Mathematics, and Culture in Everyday Life. Beyond solitary study, talking with other programmers and reading their code is more valuable than any book or course. Formal education can help—four years of college or more in graduate school grants credentials and a deeper grasp of the field—but if you dislike academia you can acquire comparable knowledge on your own or on the job, provided you remain dedicated. Remember, book learning alone will not suffice; as Eric Raymond notes, studying brushes and pigment will not make you an expert painter any more than computer‑science studies will make you an expert programmer. One of the finest programmers I ever hired possessed only a high‑school diploma, yet he built impressive software, ran his own news group, and earned enough from stock options to purchase a nightclub.

To grow, work on projects alongside other programmers. Take turns being the best and the worst on different efforts; when you lead you test your ability to inspire and direct, when you trail you observe what masters do and what they avoid because they assign those tasks to you. Study programs written by others, understand their structure, and learn how to maintain them when the original authors are unavailable. Think ahead about how your own designs will ease future maintenance. Learn at least half a dozen languages, covering different paradigms: one emphasizing class abstraction (Java or C++), one highlighting functional abstraction (Lisp, ML, or Haskell), one supporting syntactic abstraction (Lisp), one enabling declarative specification (Prolog or C++ templates), and one geared toward parallelism (Clojure or Go). Remember that computer science contains a computer; know how long an instruction takes, how fast a word is fetched from memory with and without a cache miss, how quickly consecutive disk words are read, and how long a seek to a new disk location costs. Participate in a language standardisation effort—whether on an ANSI C++ committee or deciding whether your local style uses two or four spaces—but withdraw from it promptly once you have absorbed the community’s values and motivations. All of this makes clear that reliance on books alone leaves you far short of true expertise.

My own experience illustrates the point: before my first child arrived I devoured every “How To” guide I could find and still felt like a clueless novice. Thirty months later, when my second child was on the way, I did not return to those books for a refresher; instead I leaned on the practical knowledge I had accumulated, which proved far more useful and reassuring than thousands of pages of expert advice. Fred Brooks’ essay No Silver Bullet outlines a three‑part plan for identifying and nurturing great software designers—spot top talent early, assign a career mentor, and give developing designers chances to interact and stimulate each other. Alan Perlis expressed a similar idea: everyone can be taught to sculpt, but Michelangelo would have needed instruction on what not to do; the same holds for great programmers, who possess an internal quality that transcends training. Whether that quality is innate or forged through diligent practice is debatable, but Auguste Gusteau’s counsel in Ratatouille—that anyone can cook, but only the fearless can be great—captures the spirit. Anton Ego adds that not everyone can become a great artist, yet a great artist can emerge from anywhere. So feel free to pick up a quick‑start Java, Ruby, JavaScript, or PHP book; you will gain some immediate utility. Yet you will not overhaul your life or attain genuine programming mastery in twenty‑four hours or twenty‑one days. Real progress begins when you commit to steady improvement over months and years, not when you chase the illusion of instant expertise.
