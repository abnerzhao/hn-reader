## medium

Large language models have changed how people approach tasks requiring technical knowledge. In the 2010s, someone with gaps in coding skills like CSS relied on experts or searched the internet for exact answers. Today, LLMs let anyone produce passable results by simply asking the model. This accessibility has led many to view LLMs as tools that make everyone a generalist. The idea is that anyone can ask for high-quality output—like advanced math or clean code—and get it. But this overlooks a key factor: expertise in the specific domain being asked about still makes a huge difference in how well LLMs perform. Even when everyone uses the same model, skilled users achieve better results than novices. A strong example comes from Terence Tao, a mathematician, interacting with ChatGPT about a recent problem in Jacobian Conjecture. Unlike casual users, Tao guided the model toward the solution through precise, expert-driven questions rather than vague requests.

Tao’s approach shows that domain knowledge shapes how users interact with LLMs. He communicated concisely, focusing on core ideas rather than step-by-step exchanges. The model responded more effectively because Tao signaled he was a mathematician, not someone needing basic explanations. He didn’t just accept the first answer; he challenged it subtly by pointing out inconsistencies or complexity. For instance, instead of saying ‘this is wrong,’ he might say ‘this seems overly complicated,’ prompting the model to refine its thinking. His ability to jump to alternative methods or reformulate questions relied on his deep understanding of mathematics. He could pull specific insights from the model’s output and steer it toward promising directions. This isn’t just about knowing math—it’s about knowing how to ask the right questions and recognize what a valid solution looks like. Contrast this with typical users, who might accept generic answers without scrutiny, missing better results that require refined queries.

The same principles apply beyond mathematics. In programming, someone familiar with a codebase can push an LLM to suggest better solutions than a stranger. If you understand how a system works, you recognize gaps in suggested answers or know where to apply pressure for clarity. For example, you might ask, ‘Does this approach conflict with existing logic?’ or ‘Can we simplify this using earlier methods?’ Without expertise, you might accept flawed code because the model generates it confidently. But with knowledge, you can steer the model toward solutions that align with real-world constraints. This mirrors the author’s experience with GitHub systems. They avoid asking generic software advice but instead ask specific, context-aware questions grounded in their understanding of the code. The LLM becomes a tool to refine existing knowledge, not replace it. The human’s role isn’t just to ask but to critique, adapt, and contextualize the model’s output.

The article challenges the notion that LLMs democratize expertise. While they lower barriers for basic tasks, high-value outcomes still depend on human judgment. A ‘ PPL-level’ solution might be technically correct but lack practical usefulness without domain insight. Tao didn’t just ask ‘solve this problem’—he framed questions around existing mathematical theory, leveraging his expertise to bypass irrelevant suggestions. Similarly, a programmer with system knowledge can extract efficient code by referencing past patterns, while a beginner might generate verbose or inefficient solutions. This isn’t about rejecting LLMs but recognizing their limits. The author argues that domain expertise remains critical because models generate information, but humans must filter and apply it. For tasks requiring precision or innovation, the human’s understanding of context and goals determines success. Even as models improve, the bottleneck will likely remain human—figuring out how to communicate needs clearly and critically to get desired results.

The piece touches on broader implications for AI’s role in workflows. Commenters on Hacker News shared stories aligning with this view: experts found LLMs more valuable when they could apply context, while novices struggled with generic outputs. Some skeptics questioned the premise, noting that OpenAI’s math demonstrations involved expert verification behind the scenes. The author acknowledges this but stresses that current tools still require human oversight. Despite advancements, the need for expertise suggests human skills won’t become obsolete. Instead, they’ll evolve to work alongside models. The key takeaway is that LLMs amplify technical knowledge rather than replace it. For many tasks, the struggle isn’t model capability but translating human intent into effective prompts. As models grow stronger, the ability to guide them with domain-specific insight will become an even rarer and more valuable skill.

## hard

In the 2010s, technical gaps necessitated a reliance on skilled colleagues or the hope that a specific solution existed somewhere on the internet. Today, large language models have democratized capability, allowing anyone to produce functional CSS, PhD-level mathematics, or passable professional prose by delegating the task to an AI. This shift has led to a widespread misconception that interacting with these models requires no specific skill. Because most users are interacting with the same underlying models, many 'killed prompters' are yielding results identical to novices. However, this assumption is fundamentally flawed; the most critical skill in prompting is not the manipulation of the model itself, but expertise within the domain being addressed.

A profound illustration of this phenomenon is Terence Tao's interaction with ChatGPT regarding a recent counterexample to the Jacobian Conjecture. This dialogue reveals a version of the model that is vastly different from standard interactions. Tao's efficacy stems from several subtle techniques: his messages are brief and focus on the gist rather than point-by-point rebuttals; his outputs are more concise because he signals expertise, which shunts the model into a mode of communicating with mathematicians rather than explaining concepts to amateurs. He pushes back when responses appear incorrect without direct contradiction, using nuanced observations, and he makes significant intellectual leaps himself, rarely relying on the model for direction.

Crucially, one cannot replicate Tao's success through mere mimicry of his prompting style. His effectiveness is predicated on a deep, fundamental understanding of the mathematics involved. It is his ability to extract relevant insights from multi-paragraph responses, suggest alternative formulations, and identify subtle anomalies that drives the conversation. While Tao is a mathematician, a similar principle applies to programming. A developer with a robust theory of their codebase can push an LLM much further than a novice. Familiarity allows a user to challenge the model with specific directives, such as suggesting simpler implementations or questioning how a new solution integrates with existing logic.

This reveals that system design is often dominated by concrete specifics rather than generic principles. While general understanding is useful, granular familiarity with a specific codebase provides superior leverage. Tao's ability to ask highly specific questions—asking how a particular component functions under certain conditions—is the key to wringing maximum value from the model. Without domain knowledge, one is forced to merely cling to the model's output to reach a baseline of utility. With domain knowledge, one can steer the model aggressively toward a desired, high-precision outcome.

As models grow increasingly capable, the utility of human expertise is likely to persist, as the human often becomes the bottleneck in the process. The primary difficulty lies not in the model's access to information, but in the human's ability to communicate the exact nature of the desired solution. The information is latent within the model, but extracting it requires a sophisticated human intellect. While some argue that expert intervention may not be strictly necessary for all tasks, current workflows in high-level mathematics still require expert mathematicians to verify and filter the model's suggestions, a step that cannot currently be bypassed.
