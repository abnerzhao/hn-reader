## easy

Gorton is a font everywhere in New York. I found it on keys, signs, and even space. It’s old, from 1902. It’s simple, all lines same thickness. I first noticed it on keys.

Gorton is carved into metal or plastic. This makes it last. It’s on keys, signs, and machines. It’s durable because carving doesn’t wear off. It’s called Gorton after a company.

Companies made copies of Gorton. Some called it Leroy or Wrico. They used it for signs, manuals, and even comic books. It spread beyond engraving.

## medium

In 2007, during my first visit to New York City, I photographed numerous fonts I believed deserved attention. Among them were American Typewriter, Akzidenz Grotesk, Helvetica, and Gotham, but one font remained unnoticed despite its ubiquity. That font was Gorton. Initially unfamiliar, I struggled to locate it online, as the name yielded no downloadable versions. Its true nature emerged gradually as I examined its distinctive features—unusually square proportions, a childlike yet mechanical character, and letters that seemed perpetually unstable. The G always appeared ready to tumble, while Q, P, and R stretched excessively wide. Ambiguous symbols like & and @ drew criticism in type critiques, and the lowercase C felt incomplete, lacking a proper ligature connection.

Research traced Gorton to the George Gorton Machine Co., a Wisconsin-based manufacturer of pantograph engraving equipment dating to the mid-20th century. The original Gorton Normal appeared in the company's 1952 catalog, explaining its raw, uneven aesthetic. Earlier references from 1925 and 1902 confirmed the font's remarkable age, predating Helvetica and contemporary with Akzidenz-Grotesk. Unlike conventional fonts, Gorton maintained consistent stroke widths throughout—every line carried identical thickness—and shared uniform rounded terminals. Its italic variant offered only a simple slant without refinement, while a condensed version retained near-perfect proportions.

Beyond keyboards, Gorton adorned everything from typewriter panels and office signage to subway tiles, traffic controls, and military hardware. Its durability stemmed from its physical construction: letters were carved into the underlying material rather than applied as ink or digital pixels. This approach granted exceptional longevity, surviving decades of wear unlike paper or paint. The font evolved through derivative systems like Leroy and Wrico, which adapted Gorton's principles into simpler manual lettering kits. Companies such as Keuffel & Esser and Wood-Regan expanded its reach into technical documentation, comics, and even spacecraft interfaces.

Legal recognition followed, with the U.S. military adopting Gorton as MIL-SPEC-33558 in 1968 and later incorporating it into standards. The font's journey illustrates how functional necessity shaped typographic identity—a tool designed for rapid tracer guidance became embedded in global infrastructure. Today, Gorton persists as a testament to practical design, bridging mechanical craftsmanship and modern computing.

## hard

In two thousand seven, during my inaugural journey to New York City, I acquired a brand‑new DSLR camera and devoted myself to photographing every typeface I deemed worthy of admiration. I observed American Typewriter across numerous I <3 NYC logos, witnessed Akzidenz Grotesk and Helvetica competing for dominance on subway signage, and even caught fleeting appearances of Gotham, still rising to prominence through Barack Obama’s inaugural campaign. Yet there remained one typeface entirely invisible amidst this urban typographic landscape—a single font that permeated every environment I encountered. Over the following year in New York, I traversed more than one hundred miles and captured thousands of images of nothing but one particular character set. Its designation is Gorton.

The realization that Gorton existed emerged sometime in two thousand seventeen, while I was conducting research for a monograph on the history of typing. Several older keyboards, particularly those manufactured earlier, displayed a distinctive font upon their keycaps—unusually square in proportion and a peculiar amalgamation of mechanical precision and childlike whimsy. Upon closer inspection, the strangeness became evident: the capital G seemed perpetually poised to tumble sideways, while the letters Q, P, and R bore disproportionately broad strokes. The ampersand and at symbols provoked derision in any typographic critique, and the terminal forms of C resembled grasping extensions toward unseen ligatures that never materialized. The digit characters defied convention as well—the top‑flattened three echoed common Cyrillic styling, the seven sloped downward in an unconventional manner, the four adopted a strikingly geometric profile, the six and nine exhibited an odd symmetry, and the circular o merged dangerously with the numeral zero. Across multiple keycaps, subtle variations surfaced: zeros appeared square, dotted, or slashed; the slender i occasionally carried serifs; the r and six shifted their central axes upward or downward; and a narrower variant existed specifically for lowercase usage. The initial reaction was disbelief—was this truly the origin of the term “grotesque”? A secondary thought offered reluctant acceptance: despite its apparent chaos, there was something undeniably appealing about its idiosyncratic charm.

Investigating the identity of Gorton proved elusive. The website The Font posited a connection to TT Rounds, while Identifont suggested Divulge, and my earliest hypothesis pointed toward DIN Rounded or a style reminiscent of road signage. Regardless of precise classification, the defining characteristic of Gorton was its flat R, which sharply distinguished it from Helvetica and rendered its overall geometry less rounded than even the unrounded Gotham. Within niche communities of keyboard enthusiasts, references to Gorton abounded, yet exhaustive searches returned no downloadable versions or installation instructions. My assumption that the name originated from manufacturing techniques—whether from early laboratory computers employing molded plastic components or from microcomputer era charcoaling processes—proved unfruitful. The font lacked a clear lineage, existing primarily as a nameless companion to the George Gorton Machine Company, a Wisconsin‑based enterprise that produced specialized engraving apparatus.

The company’s earliest catalog, dated two thousand fifty‑two, introduced Gorton Normal, a name that resonated with me instantly. This revelation illuminated both the literal and figurative roughness inherent in Gorton’s design philosophy. Typography frequently draws inspiration from calligraphic gestures—delicate brushwork producing nuanced curves and thoughtful interplay between thin and thick strokes—but Gorton eschewed such humanism entirely. Every stroke maintained identical thickness, rendering the typeface monolinear; every termination possessed uniform rounded terminals; the italic variation consisted solely of an oblique inclination without additional refinement; the condensed form retained alterations that felt almost perfunctory. Monolinear designs are seldom celebrated among contemporary type designers, who regard them as lacking fundamental design integrity. Nevertheless, Gorton represented a distinct approach rooted in mechanical production methods rather than artistic expression.

Further archival research uncovered that Gorton predates the commonly recognized mid‑twentieth‑century sans‑serif movement. A 1935 catalogue documented the exact same character set, followed by editions from twenty‑two and twenty‑five, and finally a specimen dating to nineteen‑zero‑two. Consequently, the font I initially associated with the emergence of Helvetica was already approaching retirement age at the moment Helvetica debuted. It shared contemporaneity with Akzidenz‑Grotesk, the first true sans‑serif typeface released three years prior to the century’s close. The 1902 catalogue remains remarkably intact, complete with telegraphic notation that underscores how stark and minimalist the original design must have appeared beside the ornate logos of modern corporate identities.

What fascinated me most was Gorton’s physical embodiment. Unlike contemporary digital fonts that exist as binary data, Gorton was a routing font intended to be carved directly into the substrate of its host medium. Early laboratory computers and eighties microcomputers employed a method wherein letters were formed from solidified plastic molds, with subsequent key construction surrounding the negative space. Each Gorton character was as permanent as the key itself, granting the legends an indestructibility that ordinary printed text cannot match. This tangible quality demanded specialized machining equipment that incorporated the font’s geometry into its operational architecture. Subsequent discoveries revealed that Gorton manifested in countless environments beyond keyboards: typewriter panels, office signage, airport nameplates, boat hulls, traffic control devices, elevator panels, subway cars, locomotives, submarines, jet fighters, nuclear facilities, and ultimately the lunar module aboard Apollo spacecraft. The ubiquity of this seemingly obscure typeface prompted speculation regarding its functional advantages.

Several factors likely contributed to Gorton’s enduring presence. The default routing font embodied a powerful psychological appeal—its ubiquity stemmed from the fact that engraving hardware shipped with a built‑in template bearing the manufacturer’s name. This phenomenon mirrored later defaults such as Arial or Calibri, where users unconsciously adopt the system’s preset aesthetic. Moreover, Gorton proved exceptionally pragmatic for an era before affordable mass‑production printing technologies existed. Before laser printers, dot‑matrix devices, and home inkjet systems, the only viable means of creating legible text on non‑permanent media involved labor‑intensive hand lettering. Carving Gorton into metal, plastic, or wood offered durability against ink smudging, paper degradation, and fire damage, effectively transforming disposable information into lasting artifacts. The permanence of routed type aligned perfectly with the constraints of pre‑digital production, allowing essential communication to persist across decades.

As technological capabilities evolved, Gorton adapted to new paradigms. A small commercial enterprise known as Keuffel & Esser Co. acquired the underlying engraving machinery and repurposed it to produce lettering sets marketed under the name Leroy. This abstraction stripped away the complexity of full routing, offering simplified tracer‑guided systems that eliminated the need for large brass alphabets weighing up to thirteen pounds. Parallel development occurred with Wood‑Regan Instrument Co., which issued a semi‑eponymous alternative termed Wrico. Both enterprises further distilled the process, replacing pantographs with direct pen‑on‑paper guidance kits that enabled draftspersons to render consistent letterforms rapidly and reliably. These innovations disseminated widely, appearing in technical manuals, comic books—including the seminal issue that introduced Wonder Woman—and even in popular culture through silkscreen applications on restaurant menus and social‑media‑friendly plastic lettering. The legal trajectory of Gorton reflects broader tensions in typographic copyright: while the font’s name could theoretically be trademarked, the shapes themselves remain unprotected in certain jurisdictions, permitting derivative works such as digital implementations and font files. Thus, the hardworking Gorton persists—not merely as a relic of analog eras, but as a versatile foundation that continues to inform contemporary graphic practice.
