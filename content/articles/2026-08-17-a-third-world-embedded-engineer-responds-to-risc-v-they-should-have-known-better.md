## easy

Some engineers argue that the RISC-V design is not elegant. They say it is too complex for cheap microchips. They believe one design cannot serve both small and large devices. They think RISC-V will win only because it is cheap, not because it is well-made.

However, RISC-V is different from other brands like ARM. With ARM, companies must pay fees and sign contracts to use different chips. This creates a wall between cheap and expensive parts. With RISC-V, the same basic design works for everything.

This openness is vital in places like Trinidad and Tobago. In many countries, shipping and licenses make electronics very expensive. RISC-V allows engineers to buy cheap parts without asking for permission.

## medium

The author works in Trinidad and Tobago, a small island nation off the coast of Venezuela. When seeking development boards, local availability often determines purchasing decisions. Free shipping options from major manufacturers rarely reach this location, forcing buyers to pay significantly more for basic components. This reality shapes the priorities of engineers in the region, where the difference between a cheap component and a moderately priced one represents a significant barrier to entry. The author argues that this economic constraint is fundamental to understanding the appeal of RISC-V technology.

RISC-V differs from architectures like ARM in how it addresses the needs of different market segments. While critics argue that the instruction set design is flawed, the author contends that the same base ISA enables everything from simple microcontrollers to complex operating systems. The author demonstrates this by building multiple projects using inexpensive RISC-V chips, including the CH32V003, which costs less than a dollar. These chips support essential functions such as web browsing, image generation, and real-time facial recognition, proving that the architecture scales effectively across cost ranges.

The author highlights several practical implementations of RISC-V hardware. The CH32H417 offers dual cores with high-speed interfaces and runs a full web browser without Linux. More powerful variants like the Baochip-1x include memory management units and support specialized operating systems. Even older chips like the OrangePi RV2 demonstrate how affordable RISC-V platforms can run modern software. By sharing these resources, developers in developing countries gain access to tools that were previously reserved for wealthy markets.

Critics often cite fragmentation as a weakness of RISC-V, pointing to extension mechanisms that divide the standard. However, the author explains that this fragmentation solves a different problem than the one facing ARM. On ARM, additional features require separate licenses and different core families, creating barriers to entry. RISC-V allows vendors to choose optional features without changing the underlying instruction set. This flexibility enables third-world engineers to experiment freely without negotiating contracts or paying royalties.

Ultimately, the author believes that RISC-V wins through accessibility rather than architectural elegance. The ability to obtain functional hardware at minimal cost, combined with an open toolchain, places embedded systems within reach of communities that previously relied on imported demonstration boards. This democratization of hardware empowers local innovation and ensures that technological progress benefits everyone regardless of geographic location.

## hard

The publication of Dmitry Grinberg’s lengthy critique of RISC‑V has ignited a vigorous debate on Hacker News and on Lobsters, yet the author remains unconvinced by the bias inherent in the criticism. After abandoning the STM32 and ARM ecosystems in favor of RISC‑V and producing a comprehensive video tutorial titled “Goodbye STM32 ARM – Meet the CH32 RISC‑V Chips That Replaced It,” the author feels compelled to challenge the notion that the architecture’s design flaws justify its rejection. The central tension lies in the assertion that the instruction set’s elegance should determine its adoption, a claim the author contends overlooks the practical realities faced by engineers in economically constrained environments.

Working out of Trinidad and Tobago, a small island nation off the Venezuelan coast, the author encounters shipping obstacles that render premium components financially prohibitive. Ordinary distributors refuse to ship one‑dollar chips to the Caribbean, forcing the author to pay between sixty and two hundred U.S. dollars for parts that competitors receive for free. A PCB manufacturer declined a potential sponsorship solely because of the destination, illustrating how logistical barriers disproportionately affect developers in developing regions. Students and educators throughout Trinidad, Nigeria, and Bangladesh share this experience, making the distinction between a ten‑cent component and a one‑dollar part far more consequential than abstract discussions about encoding efficiency or instruction set aesthetics.

At the heart of the author’s defense lies the CH32V003, a compact RV32EC microcontroller priced at approximately ten cents that embodies exactly the instruction set characteristics Grinberg criticizes. This chip offers sixteen general‑purpose registers, lacks both a hardware multiplier and a division unit, operates exclusively in machine mode, and provides only two kilobytes of SRAM alongside sixteen kilobytes of flash memory. The author has successfully deployed this core in multiple production scenarios, including a bin monitoring device equipped with a TOF sensor, an LED indicator, and an air‑tagging function, as well as an agricultural automation system that controls door operation according to scheduled timelines. Even a simple project such as a whistle switch demonstrates the versatility of the architecture for low‑cost applications.

When contrasted with the ARM ecosystem, the disparity becomes stark. The closest analogue to the CH32V003 within the ARM family is the Cortex‑M0, an entry‑level microcontroller lacking an integrated memory management unit, whereas stepping upward to the Cortex‑M7 introduces a full MMU, sufficient RAM, and support for protected multi‑process operating systems. Achieving comparable functionality requires transitioning to Cortex‑A series processors—ARMv8‑A cores capable of running Linux or the Xous microkernel—each demanding substantially greater resources and specialized debugging infrastructure. The author notes that obtaining a Cortex‑M7 evaluation board typically costs around twenty dollars, while the corresponding ARMv8‑A variant commands prices exceeding several hundred dollars and necessitates multi‑thousand‑dollar debug probes.

Grinberg characterizes the divergence between high‑end CPUs and inexpensive microcontrollers as an architectural inevitability rooted in opposing functional requirements. However, the author argues that this framing misattributes the cause to the instruction set rather than to commercial segmentation strategies. On ARM platforms, the absence of an MMU on the Cortex‑M series reflects deliberate product differentiation enforced by licensing agreements, not fundamental limitations of the RISC‑V specification. The RISC‑V privileged specification permits optional features such as supervisor mode and paging, allowing implementers to include or exclude them based on cost constraints. Consequently, the same instruction set serves both a ten‑cent chip and a high‑performance system without requiring distinct instruction modifications.

This flexibility distinguishes RISC‑V from the ARM landscape, where the boundary between microcontroller and application processor is defined by licensing contracts rather than technical necessity. The author highlights the VexRISC‑V open core as evidence of this principle, noting that Bunnie Huang constructed a secure computing platform around an open specification without negotiating royalties. Similarly, the Baochip‑1x SoC incorporates an MMU built atop an open stack, demonstrating how RISC‑V enables independent innovation across cost tiers. The resulting ecosystem allows engineers in resource‑limited settings to acquire identical instruction sets at minimal expense, fostering local development and educational opportunities that would otherwise remain inaccessible.

Ultimately, the author contends that RISC‑V succeeds not because of aesthetic superiority but because of economic accessibility combined with architectural transparency. By eliminating licensing fees and providing unified toolchains, the ISA places embedded systems within reach of communities previously dependent on demonstration boards owned by external vendors. The ability to obtain fifty units of a ten‑cent chip with free shipping and comprehensive development support exemplifies how open standards democratize technology. This approach represents a form of engineering freedom that transcends mere elegance, proving that pragmatic considerations often outweigh theoretical purity in determining technological adoption.
