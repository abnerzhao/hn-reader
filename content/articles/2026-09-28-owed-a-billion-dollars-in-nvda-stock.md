## easy

In 1993, I helped NVIDIA as an early advisor. I was given 25,000 shares of stock.

The papers said all shares would be given after one year. A letter in 1996 said only part was ready.

I forgot about the shares for many years. In 2024, I found the old papers.

The math showed I should have received all shares long ago. The missing shares, after stock splits, are now worth about one billion dollars.

Lawyers tried to help, but the time limit to claim the shares had passed. I accept this result.

## medium

I was an early advisor to NVIDIA in 1993 and was invited to join the Technical Advisory Board by Jensen, who at that time was still without his famous leather jacket. In September of that year I received 25,000 stock options, to be granted in quarterly installments so that all shares would vest one year after the grant date. This followed a meeting I had with Jensen, Curtis Priem and Chris Malachowsky on my houseboat, the SS Vallejo, in Sausalito. I had previously worked with Curtis at Sun Microsystems on the SPARCstation GX chip, which could blit polygons at about 50 000 per second, and we had ported Sense8’s VR rendering to it. Later I implemented bilinear texture mapping using the Intel i750 DVI chip, but it was my fast biquadratic texture‑mapping demo that caught Curtis’s attention and led him to bring the other founders to see it.

The biquadratic texture mapping was described in US 5796426A and later patents, and Curtis saw it as a way to differentiate NVIDIA’s first product, the NV1, from rivals. I worked casually with Curtis to port the code to their hardware and wrote the program for an Intel‑sponsored VR string‑quartet demo that ran on a prototype NVIDIA board – essentially a GX card in a PCI adapter – shown at the Guggenheim SoHo in 1993. When the NV1 finally shipped in 1995, Microsoft decided, for its own reasons, not to support quadratic texture mapping or even quads in the newly released DirectX toolkit, allowing only triangles. This decision hurt NVIDIA’s finances and prospects, prompting a large round of layoffs. By April 1996 I had moved to the Kingdom of Tonga and was involved in various internet startups when NVIDIA’s CFO wrote to say that 15 625 of my options had vested and needed to be exercised. I did so, then forgot about the matter.

In 2024, while chatting with a day‑trader friend amid endless NVIDIA news, I dug out my old documents. To my surprise the signed option agreement stated that the options were to vest over four quarters, not four years, as both NVIDIA’s CFO and their outside counsel, Cooley, had claimed in 1996. The math shows that 15 625 of 25 000 shares is 62.5 %, exactly what you would expect after ten quarters of a four‑year schedule. Under the one‑year schedule actually written in the agreement, all 25 000 shares should have vested long before that 1996 letter. After accounting for NVIDIA’s many stock splits – a cumulative 480× to date – the 9 375 shares that never vested are now equivalent to 4 500 000 shares. I therefore engaged experienced counsel, Allan Steyer of Steyer Lowenthal and Chris Burke of Korein Tillery, to investigate the discrepancy.

After about a year of exchanging letters – my attorneys and NVIDIA’s in‑house and outside counsel citing case law and trading strong language – I suggested a settlement meeting, noting my advancing age and the seemingly endless correspondence. The meeting was conducted with great professionalism, but Cooley’s reply was essentially “so sue us.” Upon reflection, my attorneys and I concluded that the statute of limitations had expired; the three‑odd years I had “sat on my rights” made it unlikely we would survive a motion to dismiss. Consequently we decided not to pursue litigation.

I share this story as a cautionary tale. In the land of the free, a company sometimes needs to honor its contractual obligations only for a short period. Has anyone else uncovered a similar vesting surprise decades later, and how was it resolved? I remain sanguine and amused, recalling the Emperor Septimius Severus’s remark: “Omnia fui, nihil expedit.”

## hard

I was invited to join the Technical Advisory Board of NVIDIA in 1993 by Jensen, who at that time was not yet known for his leather jacket. In September of that year, I was granted 25,000 stock options, specified in a series of quarterly installments so that all shares would vest upon the expiration of one year from the Grant Date. This invitation came after a meeting I had with Jensen, Curtis Priem, and Chris Malachowsky on my houseboat, the SS Vallejo, in Sausalito. I had done a fair amount of work with Curtis around 1990, when he was at Sun Microsystems and the architect of the SPARCstation GX chip. Sun was one of the companies supporting my early virtual reality company, Sense8 Corporation. The Sun GX was exceptionally fast at blitting polygons, achieving some 50,000 per second, and we had ported Sense8’s VR rendering to it. Subsequently, I implemented bilinear texture mapping using the Intel i750 DVI chip. However, what had caught Curtis’s attention in 1993, leading him to bring the other NVIDIA founders to visit me for a demonstration, was my fast implementation of biquadratic texture mapping, the details of which are contained in US5796426A and subsequent patents. Curtis understood the potential of non-linear texture mapping to differentiate NVIDIA’s first product, the NV1, from the competition.

I worked for a while, quite casually, with Curtis on porting my biquadratic texture mapping to their hardware, and I wrote the code for an Intel-sponsored VR string-quartet demo on prototype NVIDIA hardware—actually a GX card in a PCI adapter—which was shown at the Guggenheim SoHo in 1993. When the NV1 finally shipped in 1995, Microsoft decided, for reasons of their own, not to support quadratic texture mapping, or even quads, in their just-released DirectX toolkit, opting for triangles only. This decision had a devastating effect on NVIDIA’s finances and prospects, and the company laid off a large percentage of its staff. Then, in April 1996, by which time I had expatriated to the Kingdom of Tonga and was working on various internet startup schemes, NVIDIA’s CFO wrote me a letter stating that 15,625 shares of my stock options had vested and that I was required to exercise them. I did so, and then forgot all about it.

Fast-forward to 2024. I was sitting with a day-trader friend, surrounded by screens blaring news about NVIDIA, the most important company on Earth. So I went home and dug through my folder of old documents. Imagine my surprise: according to the duly signed option agreement, my options were meant to vest over four quarters, not four years, as both NVIDIA’s CFO and their outside counsel, Cooley, had asserted back in 1996. The math is clear: 15,625 of 25,000 shares is 62.5%, exactly what you would expect after ten quarters of a four-year vesting schedule. On the one-year schedule the agreement actually specified, all 25,000 shares should have vested well before that letter was even written. With the stock’s many splits—a cumulative 480x to date—my missing 9,375 shares are now 4,500,000 shares. A handsome enough pile that I engaged formidable attorneys, Allan Steyer of Steyer Lowenthal and Chris Burke of Korein Tillery, to explore the issue. These were consummate advocates, with letterheads carrying substantial gravitas.

After about a year of my attorneys and NVIDIA’s in-house and outside counsel sending letters back and forth, citing case law and blustering, I mentioned to my attorneys that, as I was growing elderly and there seemed to be no end to this exchange of letters, perhaps they could meet and settle. NVIDIA did not dispute the authenticity of the option agreement, only that my claims were long since time-barred. The meeting was held with great professionalism, but Cooley’s answer was, in essence, “so sue us.” After much soul-searching, deliberation, and gnashing of teeth, my attorneys and I concluded that the statute of limitations was against us. Because of the thirty-odd years that had passed while I “sat on my rights,” it seemed unlikely we would make it past a motion to dismiss.

I offer this in the spirit of a cautionary tale. I am sure there are lessons here for those with a more phlegmatic personality than mine. Here in the land of the free, it turns out a company only has to honor its contractual obligations for a little while. Has anyone else discovered a similar vesting surprise, decades later? And how was it resolved? I remain sanguine, and amused. As the Emperor Septimius Severus quipped: “Omnia fui, nihil expedit.”

Eric Gullichsen, September 2026
