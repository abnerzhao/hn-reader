## easy

Some people want AI to sound like humans. They ask AI to use simple words or short sentences. I see this in viral tech posts and GitHub. It seems nice but I think it’s wrong. AI is not a human. It needs to keep exact details, not just make things sound friendly. This compression loses information.

When AI follows rules to be simple, it might drop important facts. Like a filter removing details. Errors or tricky parts can get lost. Humans might read a smooth version without knowing what went wrong. Agents sharing info with each other makes this worse. A detailed error becomes a vague sentence.

Better way: Let AI keep all exact info until humans read it.

## medium

One of the clearest indicators of shifting culture and sentiment regarding AI tools is the rise of niche prompt engineering trends. On platforms like X, GitHub, and Hacker News, we are seeing an influx of instructions designed to make Large Language Models more human-friendly, such as requesting outputs in Simplified Technical English or asking them to talk as if the user has ADHD. While the desire to avoid the verbosity and quirks of typical LLM outputs is understandable, trying to fix this by humanizing the model itself is a mistake of abstraction.

The issue is that these stylistic instructions are not applied as a post-processing step; they become part of the core workload. When you instruct an agent to use short sentences, avoid jargon, and only include essential details, you are asking it to continuously compress its output into a lower-bandwidth format. This compression is inherently lossy. While the output might still read nicely, the true problem is that the raw state of the model is often the most information-dense and accurate representation available.

This becomes even more problematic when agents begin communicating with one another. If a subagent investigates a bug and turns its findings into a nice human-readable summary for a parent agent, information is lost at every step. If a subagent runs six tests, I do not want a pleasant summary saying most tests passed; I want the exact data: 5/6 PASS, the specific failure, the cause, and the reproduction steps. Human prose is excellent at smoothing over friction, but that smoothness often hides failure.

Agents fail in ways that are useful specifically because they are ugly: conflicting evidence, unresolved branches, and stack traces. Human-like prose is incredibly good at masking these issues with phrases like, 'There are a few considerations here.' I would much rather see an agent hallucinating or hitting its token limit than have its errors smoothed into a polite, confident sentence. In every other computing system, we keep the highest-fidelity representation as long as possible and only transform it at the boundary where a human consumes it.

The current trend in LLM tooling is effectively doing this backwards. This is not an argument against accessibility or personalization; if you want three-line answers, you should have them. However, it is much better to let agents maintain detailed state and exchange exact errors, schemas, and confidence levels, and then compress that data for the user at the very end. The viral trends we see now are not the end state; they are a bug report suggesting that we need to move human-friendly rendering further down the stack.

## hard

The largest indicator of cultural and emotional shifts in AI tool usage for me comes through platforms like X, viral GitHub repositories, and Hacker News. These spaces highlight a recurring trend: users increasingly request outputs tailored to specific cognitive or perceptual frameworks. I’ve observed this frequently in the form of prompts designed for individuals with ADHD—aimed at shortening sentences, eliminating redundancy, or structuring information in hyper-visible chunks—or in instructions mandating adherence to ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English. While the appeal of these adjustments is understandable, given how LLM outputs often feel unnecessarily verbose or idiosyncratic to human users, I believe this approach conflates two fundamentally different problems. The core issue isn’t that LLM outputs are unwieldy; it’s that users are misattributing style as a solution when adjusting the model’s behavior during computation rather than treating it as a post-processing concern.

What makes this approach particularly problematic is its integration into the agent’s operational workflow rather than its application after task completion. For instance, if an agent is instructed to avoid jargon, simplify technical terms, or suppress non-critical details during processing, it is not merely modifying the final output but inherently compressing information at every stage. This compression is inherently lossy. While users may not notice missing details because the remaining text still ‘reads nicely,’ the act of continuously forcing the model to operate within stricter linguistic constraints distorts its ability to retain and convey precision. ASD-STE serves as a textbook example here. Simplified Technical English was created to reduce ambiguity in documentation for human audiences, yet agents aren’t humans. The raw, unadorned state of an agent’s processing—replete with technical specificity, contextual nuances, and unfiltered data—often contains more information than any simplified version could. When these constraints are merged with operational imperatives like task accuracy, tool usage correctness, or system preservation, they create a conflict. The model is asked to prioritize human readability at the expense of its primary function: executing tasks with max information.

This dilemma becomes even starker when agents interact with other agents. Consider a subagent diagnosing a bug: it might generate a human-readable summary of findings, which a parent agent then processes into another simplified summary for the end user. If the subagent ran six tests, with five passing and one critical failure, the humanized output might reduce it to ‘Most tests passed, though one issue warrants attention.’ In contrast, a machine-focused output would expose specifics: ‘5/6 PASS, FAIL: test_cache_invalidation, CAUSE: stale key survives restart, REPRO: tests/cache_test.py:184.’ The former sacrifices actionable detail for comfort, while the latter preserves diagnostic utility. Human prose is adept at smoothing over failures—masking conflicts, unresolved states, or uncertain assumptions into palatable language. While this may feel satisfying, it obscures the agent’s actual performance. For example, if an agent is hallucinating or hitting memory limits, a softened human-facing summary might camouflage these errors. By contrast, systems like databases or compilers maintain raw, high-fidelity data structures until the final user interface layer, where formatting occurs. LLM tooling, however, increasingly shifts this backward, embedding humanization into the model’s core rather than the output’s presentation.

To clarify, this critique isn’t an argument against accessibility or personalization. If a user genuinely needs concise, simplified English or shorter responses, that’s valid. The issue lies in how these preferences are enforced. Currently, viral repos promote embedding such instructions directly into prompts, effectively rewriting the agent’s operational logic to prioritize human consumption early in the process. This misplaces the responsibility of output adaptation onto the model itself rather than positioning it as a final, contextual layer. A more robust future would separate these concerns: allow agents to retain precise, machine-centric state—exchanging schemas, diffs, error codes, confidence metrics, or provenance during internal operations. The human-facing version would then emerge only at the boundary, tailored to the user’s needs without compromising the agent’s internal fidelity.

The viral skills circulating in these communities might seem like they point toward progress, but they actually reveal a systemic misunderstanding. Prompts like ‘Talk to me like I have ADHD’ or style-specific instructions function as workarounds rather than solutions. They operate at the prompt layer, which is a temporary and fragmented fix. The durable version, in my view, is agents that operate natively in precise, machine-readable formats, reserving humanization for final output. This aligns with how other systems function: databases store structured data separately from dashboard visualizations, compilers preserve intermediate representations (IR) distinct from human-readable log files, and APIs exchange structured payloads rather than friendly summaries. By design, these systems delay human-facing abstraction until the point of consumption, preserving maximum information integrity until needed.

The fundamental flaw here is humanizing the abstraction layer itself. When users demand outputs that mimic easier human cognition—such as shorter sentences for attention spans or jargon avoidance for clarity—they’re conflating adaptability with intelligence. Agents aren’t meant to mimic human thought processes; they’re tools for processing information. Their strength lies in handling scale, consistency, and technical rigor, not in filtering their outputs through subjective human preferences. The viral trends we see now are symptomatic of a broader pattern: users attempting to solve interface-level problems at a model-level rather than refining the tools designed for them. This results in agents being forced into inefficient modes of operation, where their most valuable traits—speed, accuracy in technical domains, and capacity to handle ambiguity—are deliberately curtailed.

Ultimately, these viral repos and community-driven style shifts aren’t a step toward better AI tools but a symptom of a misaligned expectation. They reflect users’ frustration with LLM outputs, not an insight into the technology’s capabilities. The real advancement would be decoupling the agent’s computational process from its output format entirely. Imagine agents operating in a noise-tolerant, detail-rich environment, transmitting data in its rawest form—whether that’s error logs, state transitions, or critical parameters—while rendering occurs only when a human interacts. This preserves the agent’s integrity and allows humans to choose how they digest the information. Until this distinction is recognized, instructions to ‘humanize’ outputs will continue to be a recurring bug report—practical in the short term but fundamentally misguided as a technical approach.

The path forward requires a shift in how we conceptualize AI tools. Instead of optimizing the model to fit human cognitive quirks during execution, we should optimize for machine execution first, creating robust, interpretable states internally. Human language and format should be the final touch, applied only when required. This is analogous to how professional software development operates: backend systems don’t concern themselves with user interfaces until the final deployment. By adopting this layered approach, we preserve the agent’s capacity to reason, store, and process information at its highest potential, reserving humanization for the point of interaction. Viral repos, then, aren’t the future—they’re a cautionary sign of an untapped efficiency benefit being lost to convenience over correctness.
