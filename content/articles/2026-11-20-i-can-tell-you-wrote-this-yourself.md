## medium

Those were some of the first words my honours project supervisor said to me last week, when we met to discuss a progress report I'd written for the midway point of the project — a roughly 10-page report on the control of flapping-wing micro aerial vehicles, probably boring unless you're into that. I'd accidentally set the due date a week early, so after 48 hours stressed and pumping out 5,000 words, citing references and adding diagrams, I was relieved to find I'd tricked myself into being productive, with plenty of time for feedback. So when he said he could tell I'd written it myself, I wasn't sure how to take it. A compliment? An insult? Then he added: 'I've been sent a lot of reports written by AI.'

At first that shocked me. Our reports are limited to 10 pages — how hard is it to write 10 pages yourself? But then I thought about the last year of my engineering degree. Assignment documents that start with 'you are strongly encouraged to explore the use of AI,' which quickly becomes the clear understanding that you aren't just encouraged but expected to use it, and that you're disadvantaged if you don't. The hardest class of my degree was spent in a windowless computer lab, prompting an AI for eight hours a day.

The reason we've been pushed towards it? Apparently that's where the industry is going, and knowing things matters less than knowing how to prompt a machine to do the thing. Apparently the future of engineering is to become a glorified babysitter. The AI isn't going anywhere, they say, so we'd better learn to manage it. But sitting in a room telling an AI 'ignore the deadline, keep working' isn't filling me with enthusiasm. It's soul-crushing, and I don't want to outsource so much of my thinking.

In a world where people use AI to summarise books instead of reading them, and turn to it for therapy, I don't want to keep using it. As literacy and numeracy rates decline, I worry it starts with an assignment you're encouraged to do, then becomes relying on it for a 10-page report you should know back-to-front — one you'll present and defend in person — and then asking it for anything you'd normally look up or ask a friend. The internet is riddled with AI content that makes it harder to tell fact from fiction, and in a world so polarised and deliberately provoking, identifying bias has perhaps never mattered more. So I'm practically begging you: don't automate your thinking. Read the books. Write the papers. Search for answers. Ask a friend. Think about what the creators of the content you consume want you to think, and treat it like that Year 7 English class where you learned to spot persuasive techniques.

On a brighter note: I saw a tawny frogmouth up close in the middle of the day, finished my final assessment so only one semester to go, saw 'You Can See Everything' with Han and can't stop thinking about it, made Mum's scalloped potatoes when I felt homesick, and made plans to see 'Sunrise On The Reaping' next week. Happy almost-weekend!

Apparently actually knowing things doesn't matter nearly so much as knowing how to prompt a machine to do the thing. Apparently the future of engineering - at least in my field - is to become a glorified babysitter. The AI isn't going anywhere, they say, so it's best we learn how to manage it well. But sitting in a room telling an AI 'ignore the approaching deadline, we still have 8 hours, keep working' isn't exactly filling me with enthusiasm for the rest of my career. It's soul-crushing, and I don't want to outsource so much of my thinking to it. In a world where people are using AI to summarise books instead of reading them and turning to AI for therapy, I don't want to keep using it. It feels like such a slippery slope. As literacy and numeracy rates decline, I can't help but question whether encouraging us to rely on AI so heavily is a good idea. It makes me wonder: are we still thinking for ourselves at all? Because I worry that it starts with using it for an assignment when you're encouraged to, but then it turns into relying on it to write a 10-page report on a project you should know back-to-front. A project that you'll have to present and defend in person when it finishes. And then you're asking it for advice, using it to summarise books, turning to it for anything you would normally look up or ask a friend. The internet is riddled with AI content that is making it increasingly more difficult to tell fact from fiction. In a world where everything is so polarised and deliberately provoking, the ability to identify bias or motivation in the content we consume has perhaps never been more important. Being able to think for ourselves has perhaps never been more important. As the tech world continues to offer us increasingly more opportunities to automate things, I'm practically begging you to please not automate your thinking. Read the books. Write the papers.
