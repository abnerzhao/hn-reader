## easy

I still use coding assistants for personal projects. One‑shot features leave me unsatisfied. I like them for boring parts.

They help me skip tedious steps. But letting the assistant roam creates cognitive debt. I want to understand tagging on my site. I hate reading docs, yet I need to know how it works.

My fix: ask the assistant to show code in chat. I type it myself. I tell it not to edit files automatically.

Typing builds a mental map of my code. I spot errors faster. It feels like learning from books, not copying.

## medium

The use of coding assistants remains a topic of debate in personal projects. While generating entire features at once leads to uncertainty, these tools are still helpful for speeding through repetitive tasks. However, relying heavily on AI-generated code creates a heavy mental burden. Even for uninteresting work, the author insists on understanding how solutions function, rather than letting machines handle comprehension entirely. He acknowledges that reviewing large amounts of AI-produced code is common practice but finds it unenjoyable and mentally taxing, especially for personal projects where enjoyment matters more than efficiency.

The author’s solution involves manually retyping all code suggestions from the AI. He enforces strict rules in his project files to prevent the assistant from making unapproved changes. Instead, every proposed modification appears in the chat, requiring him to type it himself. This approach ensures he reviews each line, even though it slows him down. Accepting this reduced speed, he values the deeper understanding it provides. By typing code manually, he builds a clearer mental image of how different parts connect and function within the project.

Manual editing acts as a learning tool. It forces him to pause and analyze the code’s structure, detect errors, or adjust logic to match his style. This process helps him identify mistakes the AI might have made, such as poor comments or defensive coding. He also gains better spatial awareness of the codebase, knowing exactly where each feature resides. This familiarity improves future interactions with the assistant, as he can give more precise instructions based on his firsthand understanding rather than guessing from generated text.

This method echoes past advice for learning programming. When learning from books or online resources, experienced developers urged him to type out examples instead of copying them blindly. Similarly, manually entering AI-generated code replicates that educational approach. While less efficient than fully trusting the AI, the author prioritizes comprehension. He believes this habit prevents future confusion, especially as reliance on AI grows in the industry. He fears many projects will accumulate ‘cognitive debt,’ where developers no longer grasp how their code works—a risk he aims to avoid in his own work.

His approach may not maximize productivity but aligns with his goal of maintaining control over his knowledge. By typing every line, he ensures he fully grasp APIs, algorithms, and code flow. If something is unclear, he stops to research or ask the AI for explanations. This active engagement allows him to refine the code as he goes, improving readability and fixing flaws. The trade-off is slower progress compared to those who accept AI-generated code unchecked. However, for personal projects, where the joy comes from the creative process rather than the final product, this cost is acceptable.

The author concludes that deeper understanding is critical in an era of growing AI dependence. He sees cognitive debt as a looming issue, where complex systems built by many might become hard to maintain if developers forget key details. While he can’t change industry practices alone, he insists on writing code he truly understands. To him, this isn’t just a personal choice but a form of professional responsibility. By insisting on manual retyping, he ensures his work remains clear and adaptable, even as AI tools become more prevalent.

## hard

In spite of my assertions made during April, I continue employing coding assistants within my personal projects. Their application to constructing complete features in a single interaction generates dissatisfaction and cognitive disarray; yet, their utility in accelerating mundane segments of my endeavors remains genuinely enjoyable. Nevertheless, permitting these assistants to operate autonomously within my projects results in an enormous cognitive debt. While I might resent the necessity of consulting the Django reference materials to implement tagging functionality on my website, I inherently desire comprehension of the underlying mechanisms. The mere tedium of a task should not justify transferring full comprehension of its resolution to an artificial entity. Naturally, I could scrutinize every line of code generated by the LLM. This is the procedural expectation placed upon developers in this particular annum of 2026, where robotic entities submit pull requests and human counterparts undertake their examination. It constitutes a novel and audacious paradigm shift. However, I derive no satisfaction from reviewing AI-created pull requests. The process of dissecting hundreds of lines of excessively precautionary, poorly annotated, subtly erroneous code is thoroughly unpleasant. Though I might reluctantly perform this task for a professional employer—while simultaneously ensuring that entity becomes a former employer—it is fundamentally incompatible with my approach to personal projects. The primary joy derived from personal projects originates from the creative process rather than the final output. Consequently, how might one reconcile this contradiction? How can one delegate tedious labor to LLMs without surrendering both control of one’s work and cognitive faculties to automated systems?

The methodology I have devised is lamentably inefficient and potentially humorous in its counterproductivity: I direct my coding assistant to generate code through chat functionality, then execute all necessary modifications manually. These directives are embedded within every agent file across my personal projects: I demand full comprehension of each line of code integrated into these projects. No files may be created, edited, relocated, renamed, or deleted unless explicit instruction is provided. Instead, every proposed alteration must be exhibited in the chat interface, permitting me to input it manually. No commands that modify project files, install dependencies, or alter repository states may be executed unless explicitly requested; such commands must also be displayed in the chat interface for manual execution. As a seasoned developer, I do not require explanations of syntax, APIs, programming principles, or implementation specifics unless explicitly solicited. This approach enables faster execution compared to nondescript methods—or more precisely, it is faster than complete abstention from LLMs—yet I operate at a slower pace than those who wholly delegate cognitive responsibility to machines. Instead of achieving exponential productivity enhancements, I likely experience only a doubling of efficiency. However, the trade-off lies in enhanced understanding of my codebase.

By manually entering every line of code produced by the LLM into my editor, I construct a comprehensive mental representation of its functionality and integration within my existing system. If any API or algorithm remains incomprehensible, I may pause to investigate further or request the LLM to clarify the matter. The act of typing each line forces a deliberate pace, significantly increasing the likelihood of identifying fabricated information or flawed architectural choices from the LLM. I can simultaneously purify the code during this process, reorganizing structures, refining logic, and adapting it to personal preferences. Most crucially, this workflow facilitates the development of a spatial understanding of the codebase. I know precisely where each functional component resides. When subsequent modifications are required, I immediately locate the exact point of alteration. This not only expedites work within the project but also enhances future interactions with the LLM through more precise prompting and directive formulation.

When acquiring programming skills as a youth, seasoned developers frequently advised against direct replication of code into projects. When studying from textbooks, I was instructed to transcribe all examples onto my computer and verify their executable status. When learning from online resources, I was similarly directed to type solutions and implement them within my own codebase to ensure complete internalization. This manual retyping of LLM-generated code into my projects replicates that precise educational methodology. Though it may not represent the most efficient utilization of LLMs, I prioritize comprehending over maximizing throughput. Having maintained this approach for several months, its efficacy has become evident. I intend to sustain this workflow until its practicality diminishes.

I harbor concerns that the software sector is accumulating a substantial cognitive debt that will necessitate eventual redress. A future may emerge where substantial segments of our digital infrastructure remain incomprehensible to their creators. While I may lack the capacity to alter industry trajectories, I can at least guarantee full understanding of the software I contribute to the ecosystem. Any alternative would constitute professional negligence.

This practice cultivates discipline. The repetition of manually transcribing code sharpens attention to detail, exposing weaknesses in automated outputs that automated users might overlook. Thin comments or redundant error handling mechanisms become apparent through direct engagement. Furthermore, the physical act of writing code by hand creates synaptic pathways that digital replication does not. This delays cognitive processing, allowing for the identification of logical inconsistencies or inefficient patterns before they become ingrained in the project. The mental effort required to reconstruct each line also reinforces memory retention—though this comes at the cost of time.

The inefficiency of this process is undeniable. A proficient developer could potentially derive 10x speed improvements from AI when fully delegating cognitive labor. My approach yields only a 2x increase, bordering on the obsolete. Yet this is the price paid for ownership of one’s work. The alternative—allowing machines to construct entire systems without human intervention—results in a dependency where comprehension is outsourced. This is akin to following recipes without understanding their principles. The satisfaction of debugging a complex issue or optimizing a component arises precisely because the cognitive execution was mine. When the LLM ‘thinks’ for me, both the problem-solving agency and the learning experience dissipate.

The digital artifact I create through this method is not merely functional; it is deeply personal. Each line of code carries imprint of deliberate choice. Comments are not generated out of necessity but authored to explain reasoning. Variables carry intentional naming conventions that reflect understanding. This contrasts sharply with AI-generated code, which often prioritizes syntactic correctness over semantic clarity. Over time, this divergence becomes uncanny. The codebase evolves into a narrative of my technical decisions rather than a compilation of algorithmic outputs. This distinction matters when collaborating with others or maintaining the project long-term.

The potential reversal of this philosophy troubles me. If the industry adopts mass adoption of AI-generated code without concomitant emphasis on human comprehension, we risk reaching a technological singularity where even developers cannot trace the logic of their tools. This erodes the foundational skills required for innovation. My method is a form of resistance—a deliberate cultivation of technical literacy in an era where such skills are increasingly devalued. It is not about rejecting technology but about preserving the human element in its application. This small, seemingly inefficient practice may be the antidote to an unfolding crisis in software engineering.
