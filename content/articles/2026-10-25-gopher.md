I am observing a trend among seventeen-year-olds and their peers, particularly those with ADHD, who express a desire to disconnect from the world's clamor and return to simpler times. As someone from those simpler times, I feel a mixture of pride and a protective, almost territorial sentiment. Flip phones, which they perceive as simple and low-tech, represented the pinnacle of innovation for us. Not only am I aware of its existence, but I can also research online how to configure numerous features I never knew existed and would have been incapable of deciphering from the manual alone. In the past, locating someone who not only understood your topic but also possessed greater knowledge was essential. This process led me to acquire broad but superficial expertise, yet I struggled to extract the esoteric knowledge from technical documentation. Technology currently occupies a paradoxical state: it is simultaneously immensely helpful and profoundly unhelpful. Ubiquitous subscriptions, constantly evolving services, and profit-driven priorities define the landscape. Discovering a community like sdf, which operates with the ethos of bygone eras, is genuinely refreshing.

I understand the dilemma. On one hand, we cannot truly revert to the past; on the other, we must somehow reconnect with those simpler principles. I am uncertain where this leaves us. All I know is that I, personally, need to decelerate my pace.

The sentiment resonates deeply. The impossibility of a literal return does not negate the value of revisiting foundational ideas. It is not about regression but about recalibration. The tools may have changed, but the underlying desire for clarity, control, and intentionality remains. For the younger generation, this often manifests as a rejection of the overwhelming, algorithmic nature of modern digital life. Their attraction to 'simpler' technologies is not merely a nostalgic whim but a conscious effort to reclaim agency. Similarly, our own journey with technology has evolved from a struggle for basic functionality to a quest for customization and mastery. The ability to look up any feature online represents a liberation from the constraints of the past, yet it can also lead to a sense of infinite, paralyzing choice. This is the core of the paradox: we have more power than ever, but it is diluted by complexity and commercialization. Spaces that operate on principles of community, transparency, and user autonomy, like sdf, become sanctuaries. They are not relics but reminders of a time when technology served the user, not the other way around. The path forward, then, is not a step backward but a thoughtful step sideways, integrating the best of both worlds.

The need to slow down is not a call for stagnation but a call for mindfulness. It is about moving with purpose rather than speed. In a world that constantly demands more, faster, the radical act is to choose less, slower, and better. This applies equally to our technology and our lives. By acknowledging the pull of the past while accepting the inevitability of the present, we can begin to build a future that honors the simplicity we crave without sacrificing the power we have gained. The challenge is to curate our tools and our time with the same care a master chef selects ingredients, ensuring that each element serves a clear and valuable purpose, free from unnecessary noise and manipulation.
