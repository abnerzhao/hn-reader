## easy

We should own the basic services we use, like roads or water. Digital chat tools are basic services we use each day. Yet we often let one company control them completely.

For 25 years, Jabber/XMPP has been an open standard for chat. Anyone can run a server, and servers can talk together. This stops one vendor from keeping users only with that vendor.

Some new tools are open source but run by one main group. When that group changes its rules, users have no easy alternative. XMPP avoids this because many groups can make compatible software.

Treating chat as basic services means we need open standards for all. XMPP shows how this can work after 25 years of use.

## medium

Infrastructure belongs to everyone. Highways, railways, and water supplies require large efforts across whole countries. Even owning a home can mean joining a cooperative or using city-owned housing. Many criticize how foreign companies dominate digital services. Europe has historically treated American technology as part of a shared 'we', though this view should have been questioned earlier. Companies are not allies, but the bigger issue is that Europe never considered digital services infrastructure seriously.

The Internet was originally built around open standards. Data center operators can choose different suppliers for servers, switches, and routers, creating flexibility. If one company fails or changes direction, new hardware can be sourced elsewhere. Communication tools are often overlooked despite being essential. Privacy advocates highlight Signal, Wire, and Threema as better choices than big tech companies. However, these apps still run walled gardens with no exit option. Open-source software helps ensure programs are not spyware, but it does not protect against a company closing its servers.

Standards need multiple independent implementations to work properly. Organizations like the IETF, W3C, and ISO create frameworks where different groups collaborate. These bodies prevent any single company from dominating. XMPP, originally called Jabber, began over twenty-five years ago. Its extensible nature allows ongoing evolution through extensions managed by the XMPP Standards Foundation.

Modern XMPP includes features like channel binding to prevent attacks. The protocol continues to evolve with experimental extensions. Some projects like Element chose not to follow XMPP standards directly, instead creating their own Matrix platform. This gives tighter control but limits interoperability. Other projects like JMAP show how standards can improve through collaborative processes.

Digital communication deserves the same treatment as physical infrastructure. Self-hosting should be possible, whether individually or through cooperatives. Collective ownership models can offer similar benefits to private homes. Adopting open standards is essential for true digital independence. XMPP represents one such solution, offering a foundation for resilient, interoperable communication systems.

## hard

Infrastructure ownership is a principle that enjoys broad assent, yet the collective “we” it invokes varies with the domain under consideration. Highways, railways, bridges and ports demand nation‑scale coordination, whereas water provision is typically delegated to municipal authorities. The aspiration to possess infrastructure also contracts to the personal scale, epitomised by the widespread desire to own one’s dwelling, although such possession need not be organised individually; cooperative or municipally‑owned housing can confer comparable advantages. Critiques of China’s neo‑colonial infrastructural outreach and the refusal to cede water supplies to corporations like Nestlé illustrate the moral weight attached to retaining control over essential services, while landlords remain a perennial object of public disfavour. Europe, by contrast, has long failed to apply the same infrastructural scrutiny to digital services, implicitly subsuming American tech giants within a putative “we” that only began to falter under the Trump administration, a skepticism that ought to have prevailed much earlier. Corporations are not allies; the deeper impediment lies in Europe’s omission of digital services from the infrastructure category altogether.

Under capitalist regimes profit‑driven firms inevitably participate in the construction and operation of infrastructure, but they must be rendered easily replaceable. Commissioning a firm to build a road is acceptable, yet half a century later the ability to contract a different entity for maintenance and repair is essential. The same logic applies to power‑grid transmission: hiring a company to erect and operate the backbone is permissible, but monopolistic ownership of the entire grid is intolerable; instead, myriad smaller actors must be able to interconnect and interoperate within the network. This necessity for vendor independence is readily grasped even by those who never manage data centres, where heterogeneity of hardware sources—servers from one vendor, switches from another, routers from a third, linked to a backbone supplied by yet another—ensures resilience against corporate obsolescence or hostile pivots. The communication layer, however, often escapes this rigorous appraisal despite communication ranking among humanity’s most fundamental activities after respiration, nourishment and reproduction.

Digital rights advocates frequently herald Signal, Wire and Threema as exemplars of ethically superior messaging platforms relative to the hegemony of Big Tech. What many privacy enthusiasts overlook is that these services nevertheless operate as walled gardens devoid of genuine escape routes; they do not federate, nor do they permit migration to alternative providers. Neither Signal’s remuneration of its chief executive nor its reliance on Amazon Web Services constitutes an intrinsic malfeasance, yet the absence of a hedge against future misconduct leaves users vulnerable should the provider terminate service or withdraw from European jurisdictions. Open‑source licensing mitigates the risk of covert surveillance and guarantees sound end‑to‑end encryption, but it does not safeguard against provider shutdown or operational cessation. Consequently, open‑source alone fails to satisfy the infrastructural criteria we ought to impose on our digital communication tools.

To attain the standards we set for ourselves, systems must be engineered such that self‑hosting is structurally feasible without being obligatory, mirroring the dual possibilities of individual home ownership and cooperative housing arrangements. Digital communication can and ought to emulate the benefits inherent in both models, a feat achievable only through the adoption and enforcement of open standards. The Extensible Messaging and Presence Protocol (XMPP), originally christened Jabber, embodies precisely such a standard. Its genesis predates contemporary political currents by more than a quarter of a century, tracing back to an open‑source community effort that was later submitted to the IETF and formalised as RFC 6120. Standards achieve interoperability and vendor independence precisely because they emerge from collaborative bodies that prevent any single corporation from imposing exclusionary or advantageous specifications.

Prominent standards‑developing organisations—ISO, IETF, W3C and the Unicode Consortium—provide the framework wherein disparate stakeholders negotiate specifications through mutual scrutiny, testing and revision. A vendor merely publishing an API for others to consume differs fundamentally from a consensus‑driven standard forged within an SDO, where competing interests, security researchers and independent developers collectively vet the protocol. Element, formerly Riot/NewVector, illustrates the former path: although its instant‑messaging client offers self‑hosting and federation reminiscent of XMPP‑based solutions, it elected to expose its own API under the banner Matrix and retains tight doctrinal control over any modifications, with leadership positions in the Matrix Foundation overwhelmingly occupied by present or former Element employees, rendering external contributions to the specification notoriously arduous.

The divergent trajectories of JMAP and Matrix illuminate the practical consequences of these approaches. JMAP, conceived as a modern successor to IMAP and SMTP Submission, originated within Fastmail before being shepherded through the IETF working group process; the protocol emerged measurably refined and now boasts at least three independent server implementations alongside a plethora of client applications. Matrix, despite emerging circa 2014, remains tethered to a single dominant reference implementation, with a nascent alternative struggling to gain traction; the reference implementation’s resource intensity renders self‑hosting prohibitive for many smaller organisations and individuals, prompting Element to market closed‑source performance‑enhancing plugins. In contrast, XMPP’s extensibility—signalled by the “X” in its acronym—allows the protocol to evolve via XMPP Extension Protocols (XEPs) administered by the XMPP Standards Foundation, which supplies the SDO scaffolding for developers to propose and ratify extensions without authoring them itself.

Adoption of XEPs has not always proceeded smoothly; Stream Management (XEP‑0198), vital for averting message loss on mobile links, attained stability in 2009 yet only saw widespread deployment around 2014‑2015, lagging behind the 2007 iPhone release and the 2008 HTC Dream. OMEMO (XEP‑0384), XMPP’s answer to industry‑standard end‑to‑end encryption, gathered momentum from 2016 onward, three years after Edward Snowden’s revelations propelled E2EE into public consciousness. The maturation of mobile XMPP is chronicled in works such as “The (Sad) State of Mobile XMPP in 2014” and its 2016 successor, underscoring that mere specification existence is insufficient; robust, preferably independent implementations are indispensable. The XSF now tracks implementation status for each XEP, guiding promotion from experimental to stable, facilitating interoperability testing, and enabling end‑users to locate clients matching their feature requirements. Contemporary clients like Dino on Linux and Conversations on Android rival proprietary counterparts, offering recent additions such as emoji reactions, cross‑device read‑state synchronisation and time‑zone indicators to avoid nocturnal disturbances, alongside channel binding—a defence against certain man‑in‑the‑middle attacks highlighted after a state‑sponsored assault on a public XMPP provider.

Looking ahead, the XMPP community is actively refining message replies, gallery‑style multi‑image sharing and OAuth support, all backed by experimental XEPs awaiting sufficient field experience before graduation to stable status. Concurrently, discussions persist regarding an RFC update that would re‑introduce the protocol to the IETF as “XMPP 2.0”. Instant messaging is far from monolithic; a team‑oriented messenger may necessitate a feature set distinct from one designed for familial or friendly interaction, yet the XMPP standard furnishes the common ground upon which developers can craft specialised clients without reinventing the protocol layer. The protocol’s endurance is remarkable: it continues to attract contributors younger than the specification itself, having outlasted venture‑backed startups, proprietary platforms and entire technological cycles. This longevity furnishes the resilience requisite in turbulent eras, positioning XMPP as an anchor, a backbone and the very infrastructure that digital sovereignty advocates have long sought but perhaps overlooked, having lain in plain sight for over twenty‑five years.
