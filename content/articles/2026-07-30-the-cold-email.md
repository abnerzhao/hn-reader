## easy

I was waitlisted at a school. My dad made me write an extra essay. It helped me get accepted. Few schools take waitlisted people, so it worked. I also emailed a company, GitHub. They hired me after we met. I didn’t know why, but it happened.

Six years ago, I sent a message on Twitter. It led to owning soccer clubs. I later invested in sports and tech. It became a big part of my life. I wouldn’t have believed it a decade ago.

Most cold emails don’t work. But trying helps. Be real and kind. Even if few succeed, it’s worth a shot. You might help someone or get help.

## medium

I was waitlisted at Carnegie Mellon, which felt like being the first loser – good enough to be talked about but not good enough to get in. My dad insisted I send an extra essay the next day, along with other material about my senior year and the tutorial site I had built, Good‑Tutorials. I thought it was silly, but I did it anyway and got in shortly after. I later learned that CMU admits only about 1‑10 % of its waitlist, so the outreach really mattered.

In 2010 Chris Wanstrath tweeted that he was looking for junior developers who knew Ruby and Java. I sent a cold email despite feeling intimidated, because GitHub then hired mostly people they had already met through open source. A couple of weeks later we met at Hotel Utah and they hired me on the spot. I still wonder why they chose me when there were stronger programmers available, but the company was barely breaking even, so a $60 k junior salary was a low‑risk experiment.

Six years ago I sent a cold direct message on Twitter and ended up owning part of a soccer club. That single DM opened a path to ownership in another club in Serie A and led to many investments in sports‑tech, as well as professional and volunteer work in the sport. It has become the main focus of my life, something I could not have imagined a decade ago.

Of course, not every cold outreach works. I have written many emails and DMs that disappeared without a trace, and I rarely remember them. This is the opposite of how a gambler recalls only the big wins; we tend to forget the awkward chats and forgotten messages while the successes stay vivid. Still, the point is simple: you cannot win unless you try. Reach out, be genuine, show real interest, and accept that most attempts will feel strange but are usually worth a shot.

I try to pay the favour forward. Whenever I run a team I accept cold résumés and emails, just as others did for me. I also invest in startups I have never met personally through cold pitches to my profile. This expands my network and helps dismantle blind spots. Remember that today you may be sending a cold email, and tomorrow you might be someone else’s cold outreach. So, with respect, restraint and good‑natured intention, go ahead and say hi when it makes sense – it could be good, it could be great, and you might help someone along the way.

## hard

I was waitlisted at Carnegie Mellon, a status that felt like being the first loser: good enough to be mentioned in conversations about admission, yet clearly not sufficient to secure a place. My father insisted I send an additional essay via next‑day air, detailing the work I had done on Good‑Tutorials, which at the time was the largest tutorial website on the planet, along with other material from my senior year. I thought the effort was pointless and a waste of time, believing I had already conveyed everything, but my father persisted, arguing that it could not hurt to make my desire unmistakably clear. Shortly thereafter I was admitted; only later did I learn that Carnegie Mellon typically admits between one and ten percent of its waitlist, making the outcome far rarer than I had initially supposed.

In 2010 Chris Wanstrath posted a tweet seeking junior developers familiar with Ruby and, reluctantly, Java. GitHub then operated as an especially cliquish startup, having hired almost exclusively people the founders had previously worked with or encountered through open‑source contributions. I was unknown to them, and the prospect of sending a cold email felt daunting. A couple of weeks later we met at the Hotel Utah, and they offered me a position on the spot. I remain unsure why they chose me when many more proficient programmers were available; in retrospect the company was essentially breaking even each month, so a $60 000 junior salary represented a low‑risk experiment. Nevertheless, I had reached out and luck favored me.

Approximately six years ago I dispatched a cold direct message through Twitter that eventually gave me a stake in a soccer club. That single DM set off a chain of events that led to ownership interest in another club, this time in Serie A, and sparked a series of investments in sports‑technology ventures as well as extensive professional and volunteer work within the sport. Over time this trajectory has become the central focus of my life, a development I would have found inconceivable a decade earlier. It illustrates that it is never too late to change direction.

I am aware that the narrative of my successes invites accusations of survivor bias; I have indeed enjoyed an unusually fortunate run, and cherry‑picking positive outcomes does not predict future performance. Yet I also recognize the multitude of cold outreach attempts I have made that yielded no response and altered nothing in my trajectory. I have contacted people I admired, acquaintances met only once, individuals who shared interests, and I am certain many of those messages vanished without trace. I do not retain clear memories of those failures, just as a gambler tends to recall the size of the pots lost while forgetting the occasional windfall. Conversely, the episodes that succeeded—awkward encounters with CEOs, forgotten emails from years past—stand out vividly in my recollection.

The essential lesson is that one cannot win without taking the initiative; you must skate to where the puck is headed, akin to buying a lottery ticket. Reaching out can feel awkward, weird, and strange, but it is usually worth the attempt. The usual caveats apply: be genuinely interested in the other person, avoid manipulative tactics, and give a sincere impression. I receive a substantial volume of cold outreach myself and can quickly discern whether the sender is trying to exploit me or is honestly seeking a real connection.

Even though warm referrals remain an excellent hiring method, I have resolved that, should I ever run a company, I would always consider cold résumés and emails, viewing it as a way of paying forward the assistance I once received. The same principle applies to unsolicited pitches to my profile on Signed: I have invested in numerous startups with which I had no prior personal connection. Such openness broadens one’s network, mitigates blind spots, and is simply the right thing to do. Moreover, it is useful to remember that while you may be the sender of a cold message today, you could easily become the recipient tomorrow; life rarely follows a strictly linear path.

Thus, on occasion, with respect, restraint, and a good‑natured, genuine effort, go ahead and reach out when it seems appropriate. The result could be merely good, or it could be great. And, whenever possible, lend a hand to others.
