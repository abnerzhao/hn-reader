## easy

Tech workers often feel sad or stuck. Some stop working and start hobbies like knitting or painting. On a train, a man showed excitement by knitting a hat. Most tech people, however, feel trapped by work. They ask if their jobs matter. Many dream of quitting to farm or teach surfing. This shows they doubt their careers.

Work used to mean finding purpose. Now, tech jobs focus on efficiency, not meaning. People work long hours on tasks others could do with AI. AI writes reports, builds websites, and plans strategies. Workers worry if their work is real or just imitation. This makes them feel disconnected from their jobs.

The worry is worse now because AI does more.

## medium

On a morning train, I watched a young man in slacks and a dress shirt, earbuds in, talking about EBITDAs and ARR. When the train stopped, he pulled out two knitting needles and a ball of pink yarn. He was making a winter hat for a niece, and for the first time that morning his eyes lit up with pride and excitement.

That moment reflects a growing trend among knowledge workers. Many are turning to pottery, painting, crochet, or dreaming of living off the grid. These conversations reveal an underlying existential angst: a sudden sense that the work they do feels pointless, a feeling that wakes them each morning and threatens to swallow them in their office chair.

The phenomenon is called workism. It replaces the deep sense of vocation—teachers, nurses, firefighters—with jobs in finance, consulting, or tech that offer prestige but little altruistic upside. David Graeber’s idea of ‘bullshit jobs’ shows that slide decks for projects that never launch or endless debates over features can feel meaningless, eroding the human need for purpose.

Artificial intelligence is accelerating that erosion. Knowledge workers are becoming managers of AI agents that draft strategies, build websites, and write reports in seconds. The work becomes an abstraction, a spectacle where the output is produced by a machine and the human feels only a distant overseer. This distance threatens the very value that keeps people in the office.

What remains valuable is the messy middle—the collaboration, debate, and shared struggle that gives work its meaning. Experience‑first workers, often artists or volunteers, thrive when they can explore, create, and connect with colleagues. If AI removes those interactions, the creative spark that fuels high‑quality output may vanish, leaving a workforce that is efficient but empty.

## hard

On a recent morning commute, a young man sat across from me on a train, lost in the monotone recitation of EBITDAs, margin expansion opportunities, and ARR. For half an hour, he spoke of nothing but corporate metrics until the train's arrival signaled the end of his monologue. As the other passengers began to disembark, he reached into his bag, not for a business book or a gadget, but for two long knitting needles and a mound of pink yarn. As he explained he was making a hat for his niece, a glint of genuine pride and excitement finally appeared in his eyes. This sudden shift from corporate jargon to analog creation highlights a growing trend among knowledge workers: a yearning for pottery, painting, or crochet—a desire to do something tangible in a world of abstraction.

This shift is not merely a change in hobbies; it is an expression of an underlying existential angst. In coffee shops and dim bars, professionals increasingly share dreams of disappearing, living on a farm, or going off the grid. These are not idle fantasies but visions of escape born from a fundamental doubt about the purpose of knowledge work itself. This disillusionment is infectious, spreading through networks of colleagues who all feel the same drop in motivation and the same mounting anxiety about the future. They find themselves asking the most unsettling of questions: What is the actual point of all of this?

We are witnessing a period of disruption that feels uniquely existential. While knowledge workers have weathered recessions, outsourcing, and automation before, the current anxiety is different. Even those most insulated from economic upheaval—highly paid executives and senior professionals with decades of institutional knowledge—are expressing uncertainty about their futures. There is a palpable sense that the very nature of their work is being called into question, a sentiment that carries significant weight as an entire class of workers begins to lose faith in the careers they once prioritized.

This phenomenon is closely tied to 'Workism,' a trend where workers seek fulfillment, community, and meaning from their careers in the same way previous generations sought it from religion. For many, the office has become a secular church where they seek to find themselves. Historically, people pursued vocations—callings that emphasize skills, values, and social impact, such as teaching, nursing, or firefighting. These roles provide a sense of purpose and connection to the community, offering rewards that transcend mere financial compensation, even on the most difficult days.

In contrast, many modern graduates dedicate their lives to finance, consulting, or technology—sectors that are demanding and complex but often lack any intrinsic altruistic upside. Much of the work involves navigating bureaucracy, performing administrative tasks to move money between entities, or optimizing details that no one will ever notice. In these environments, work can feel like a series of 'bullshit jobs'—tasks that seem to serve no meaningful function, such as building slide decks for unlaunchable projects or engaging in promotion-driven development that prioritizes optics over quality.

The rise of artificial intelligence is poised to shatter the illusion of Workism. Knowledge work has always been abstract, often structured in layers of roles that support other roles until the center is lost. Traditionally, the human element—the flesh-and-blood creativity and thought poured into a task—provided the work with life, even if its purpose was questionable. Now, AI agents are increasingly capable of executing everything from data collection to full corporate strategies. This further abstracts the worker from the work, potentially turning humans into mere managers of automated armies, stripping away the last vestiges of direct human experience.

This abstraction echoes Guy Debord's concept of 'the spectacle,' where life is no longer lived directly but is perpetually mediated through representations. Workism functions as a microcosm of the spectacle, where appearing busy and important is prioritized over actual impact. As AI takes over the execution of tasks, the medium replaces the experience entirely. A worker no longer builds the pitch; they merely write the prompt that tells an AI to build it. This shift threatens to dismantle the very structures of the organizations that Workism relies on to maintain its sense of importance.

The true casualty of this technological shift may be the 'essy middle' of collaboration and community. Many executives view AI as a way to eliminate the need for human interaction, envisioning an efficient, assembly-line version of knowledge work. However, for many employees, the value of a job lies in the shared struggle, the debate, and the relationships built during the process. If work becomes an optimized, automated process devoid of human error and interaction, it loses the very elements that make it engaging and, ultimately, sustainable for the human spirit.
