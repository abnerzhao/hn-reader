## easy

I saw a GitHub thread about chess engine work. It made me wonder why hobby groups dislike LLMs. These groups include OSDev, LangDev, and others. The thread didn’t answer much but started my thinking. People in these areas often see LLMs as a bad shortcut.

These groups care about learning, not just results. They value deep knowledge and skill-building. Using LLMs to make code fast feels like cheating to them. Early tries with LLMs often fail because users don’t understand the tools well. Angry members then spread bad ideas.

In these circles, respect comes from long effort. Sharing good code or solving hard problems slowly builds trust. LLMs might help experts, but for learners, they take away the work.

## hard

The author encountered a GitHub thread centered on chess engine development that sparked reflection on a recurring tension within hobby programming circles: their growing aversion to large language models (LLMs). While the thread itself offered limited direct insight into resolving the question, it served as a catalyst for broader consideration. Similar resistances have surfaced in niche communities such as OSDev, LangDev, TxtDev, EmuDev, RLDev, the demoscene, and code golfing forums. The prevailing sentiment across these groups centers on the idea that the knowledge cultivated within them is the core achievement, and LLMs represent a fundamental misunderstanding of their goals. These communities operate under an implicit 'not all…' framework, acknowledging exceptions but emphasizing that their defining characteristic is the deliberate, labor-intensive mastery of domain-specific skills. To them, the process itself—whether refining algorithms, debugging obscure hardware interactions, or crafting minimalist code—is the primary value, with functional outputs often treated as secondary. This ethos is rooted in a belief that true expertise cannot be shortcut, and reducing complex tasks to LLMs fundamentally undermines the communal standards of depth and rigor.

Within these circles, respect is not granted lightly. It is earned gradually through sustained participation, the exchange of intricate codebase insights, and demonstrated curiosity that transcends surface-level solutions. Shared knowledge is not merely informative; it is performative, a testament to years of grappling with challenges that defy algorithmic resolution. For example, a contributor to a retro computing project might spend months optimizing a 16-bit assembler to fit within 2KB of memory, with each tweak requiring deep architectural understanding rather than relying on probabilistic text generation. The author argues that LLMs, while potentially useful as tools for seasoned practitioners, risk becoming substitutes when used to bypass this process. The phrase 'force multiplier' is employed to describe how an expert might leverage an LLM to accelerate specific stages of work, such as drafting boilerplate or exploring theoretical frameworks. However, in communities where the act of constructing knowledge is sacred, even this approach clashes with cultural norms. Generating a final product via an LLM in such contexts is perceived not as efficiency but as an erosion of the craft itself, reducing years of accumulated skill to a superficial transaction.

The hostility toward LLMs in these niches often stems from two interrelated factors: the technical limitations of practitioners engaging with them and an entrenched cultural resistance to perceived shortcuts. Early adopters within these communities who experimented with LLMs frequently lacked the contextual fluency required to apply their outputs meaningfully. For instance, a developer attempting to generate a custom kernel module using an LLM might produce syntactically correct code that fails to address hardware-specific constraints, leading to frustration among peers who value precision. Simultaneously, a subset of community members views LLM usage as inherently unethical, conflating it with academic dishonesty. This dual pressure—between those seeking rapid progress and purists insisting on labor-intensive mastery—creates a volatile environment. The author notes that these communities have historically embraced gatekeeping behaviors, prioritizing incremental progress over efficiency. A mention of the Kool-Aid man—a reference to a pop-culture trope about sudden, disruptive change—is made to illustrate how adopting LLMs might be framed as an opportunistic, almost reckless, bid to gain visibility rather than build sustained expertise.

The author further elaborates that the resistance is not merely philosophical but pragmatic. These communities operate under the assumption that their fields are inherently complex, requiring deep, often painful, engagement to achieve reliability or elegance. For example, in the demoscene—a subculture focused on pushing graphical and audio hardware to its limits—developers spend years mastering low-level optimizations to create visual or audio effects that avoid compression artifacts. Introducing an LLM into this workflow risks producing code that fails to account for the idiosyncratic behaviors of vintage systems, rendering the output not just less efficient but potentially useless. The author contends that this dynamic is not unique to technical fields but reflects a broader human tendency to valorize struggle. By outsourcing problem-solving to an AI, members of these groups fear they are abandoning the very principles that define their identity. Moreover, the author observes that even experts are not immune to LLMs’ flaws, as their underlying training data may lack the granularity needed for niche domains. A chess engine developer using an LLM to simulate piece movement might inadvertently mirror common, suboptimal strategies rather than innovating beyond them, illustrating how expertise alone does not guarantee immunity to AI’s limitations.

The narrative voice of the author is introspective, framing these observations as part of an evolving personal perspective on LLMs. They reference a secondary piece titled 'LLMe and Mind the van Emden Gap' to suggest that their views are part of a larger discourse rather than isolated critique. The conclusion reiterates the fundamental conflict: hobby programming communities exist in a space where the journey matters as much as the destination, if not more. LLMs, while technically capable of accelerating certain tasks, are seen as antithetical to this ethos. The author does not advocate for outright rejection but cautions against their uncritical adoption in contexts where the process of learning and creation is inseparable from the outcome. The closing lines underscore this tension, warning that while LLMs may offer practical benefits to experts, their integration into these specialized communities risks diluting the cultural and intellectual work that defines them. The Kool-Aid man metaphor is revisited to emphasize the perceived abruptness of LLM adoption as a disruptive force, contrasting with the measured, iterative progress these groups historically prioritize.
