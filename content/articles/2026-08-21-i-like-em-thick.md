## easy

I used to hate books and art. I thought great stories were fake. A painting with music on a man’s butt taught me depth matters. It’s not what you see. It’s hidden layers. My teachers never showed me this. They made me think it was easy. But it isn’t. You need care to find treasure inside.

Thickness means layers. Like old art that shows work. Thin art is fake. It looks real but isn’t. A children’s book hid clues. You must reread to see. Thin books make big claims with no proof. They break when checked. Old stories last because they work. New AI art is quick. But people still want real movies. Because thickness lasts.

These are key points.

## medium

I used to believe that great literature was a lie invented by English teachers. I thought famous books were just homework assignments designed to fill time. Later I realized this belief was wrong. True greatness exists, and it lives in works called thickness. Thick art responds to your attention. The longer you engage with it, the more you discover. This responsiveness can be addictive enough to tempt people to force literature onto students.

Greatness is difficult to define, but thickness is clear. It rewards careful reading and keeps challenging the casual observer. When I entered a dark cave expecting treasure, I found only emptiness. Without proper preparation, even rich works feel hollow. I needed guidance to appreciate their depth.

One example involves The Garden of Earthly Delights by Bosch. This painting contains strange scenes and riddles throughout. A student discovered music written on a man's butt. Experts later clarified that this music was not meant to be played—it was intentionally unplayable. Bosch included it as a warning: secular music led to eternal torment. The butt music became a warning about the dangers of mixing art with profanity.

Thickness comes from several sources. First, artists often discard failed drafts, leaving traces of their struggle. Second, great works give readers freedom to add personal meaning, like cracking an egg to reveal the filling. Third, complex narratives require sustained effort to fully understand. Finally, age adds weight—works that survive centuries carry the wisdom of generations.

Thin art offers no depth. A self-help book claiming you are built to perform under pressure sounds convincing at first glance, but closer examination reveals empty promises. Its advice crumbles under scrutiny. Real substance requires layers of truth that cannot be reduced to simple statements.

Classic works endure because they have stood the test of time. They contain genuine insight that continues to resonate. Modern content may fade quickly, but timeless art remains valuable. This is why we should continue reading older works—they represent humanity's best achievements and deserve our attention.

## hard

I owe every English teacher a sincere apology. For years I believed that the so‑called great literature was nothing more than a cruel joke, a punishment for being a teenager. I thought those books had no special quality and that the whole idea of a 'great' work was just a vanity project for former English majors. I was wrong. There is a real concept of greatness, and more specifically, a concept of thickness. Great fiction, and great art in general, unfolds only when you give it your full attention. The more time you spend with it, the more you get out of it.
