## medium

In June 2026, two iconic Auckland landmarks closed within hours of each other, marking a sharp decline in the city's music culture. Flying Out, a record store on Karangahape Road, shut after 11 years of operation. Necker of the Woods, a basement venue crucial to Auckland's electronic music scene, closed due to debt but was revived by community support. Over 3,000 people contributed $150,000 to help it reopen. This recovery was exceptional in a period of widespread losses. Verona, a longstanding venue, went into liquidation after 34 years, while other businesses like Charlie's and Bar Celeste had already closed. The Others Way Festival, a key local event, canceled indefinitely. Despite these closures, the music scene itself remained active, with live performances, club nights, and festivals continuing to thrive. The issue, however, was not the absence of music but the lack of attention from media outlets covering these developments.

The disappearance of music journalism in New Zealand became evident as the industry lost its primary voices. When the NZ Herald ended its entertainment section, dedicated writers vanished. Long-standing magazines like Rip It Up and Real Groove no longer existed, and there were now fewer than five full-time music critics nationwide. Chris Schulz, a journalist who tracked this decline, noted that New Zealand had fewer music journalists than many could count on one hand. This mirrors a global trend where publications like Pitchfork were restructured or downsized. Local arts coverage nationally stood at 13 percent, far below sport's 25 percent, with non-film, non-music, and non-TV arts receiving less than 3 percent. In New Zealand, the scale of this decline was more severe due to its smaller creative sector base. Despite the scene's vitality, the absence of media reporting created a void where stories about venue closures, artist signings, or cultural shifts went unnoticed.

Economically, New Zealand's music industry remained strong even as its media coverage waned. In 2023, it contributed $451 million directly to the GDP, a significant rise from previous years, and $901 million when indirect effects were included. Live performance revenue surpassed pre-pandemic levels, reaching $329 million. Yet artist visibility plummeted: local musicians claimed only 9 percent of streaming, download, and physical sales revenue in 2024. The official Top 50 singles chart featured just one New Zealand track. Radio stations also played limited local content, with only two exceeding 20 percent. This disconnect meant promising artists struggled to get noticed, booked, or paid, as their work lacked promotion. The problem was not a lack of talent but a lack of coverage. A vibrant scene existed, but it operated without a public narrative, risking its own erasure.

To counter this, Propel emerged as a platform designed to restore the music scene's visibility. Launched to fill the media gap, it has published over 225 articles focused on electronic music in Aotearoa, including event coverage, artist features, and scene analysis. This output, while not matching the output of past full-time publications, demonstrates consistent, dedicated reporting. Propel does more than document; it provides tools to creators. Artists gain free access to profiles, press kits, booking pages, and visibility tools, resources traditionally controlled by labels or agencies. The platform emphasizes local connections within a global context, recognizing that Auckland DJs or Christchurch producers engage with international audiences. Its success is evident in partnerships formed through its coverage, such as the AUM New Year's Festival discovering Propel via its event highlights. The goal is to turn visibility into opportunity, ensuring artists gain bookings, press coverage, and career growth through Propel's efforts.

## hard

In the third week of June 2026, two devastating announcements landed on Karangahape Road within hours of each other. Flying Out, the record store that had anchored the strip for eleven years, announced its closure. Shortly after, Neck of the Woods, the basement venue that had launched a decade of Auckland electronic careers, posted that after eleven years of battling mounting debt, it had reached its end. The community refused to accept the second announcement; nearly 3,000 people, including members of The Beths, contributed $150,000 to a Givealittle page in under a week, ultimately securing the venue's reopening in July. It was a rare victory in a devastating period of decline. Verona entered liquidation in April after thirty-four years; Charlie's closed in February; Bar Celeste closed the year prior; and the Others Way Festival, the signature street festival of K Road, was cancelled in May for the foreseeable future.

Before the venues began to vanish, the people tasked with documenting them had already departed. The statistics are stark. When the NZ Herald still ran Time Out, its Thursday entertainment magazine, it maintained a dedicated team of writers; now, that magazine and those jobs are gone. Iconic publications like Rip It Up and Real Groove, which documented decades of New Zealand music, are long dead. The reality is that the number of music journalists remaining in Aotearoa can be counted on one hand, and there is no longer a full-time music critic at any major publication in the country. Research by Creative New Zealand found that arts and culture receive approximately 13 percent of total media coverage in Aotearoa, compared to roughly 25 percent for sport, while coverage of art forms outside of film, music, and television sits at barely 3 percent. This is not merely a local phenomenon, but the local edge of a global collapse, exemplified by the folding of Pitchfork into GQ in 2024. New Zealand simply reached this breaking point sooner and more severely because the market was small to begin with.

Crucially, the music scene itself has not collapsed. The problem is the loss of its mirror. The New Zealand music sector contributed $451 million directly to GDP in 2023, rising from $350 million the previous year, and exceeded $900 million when flow-on effects are included. Live performance revenue reached $329 million, surpassing pre-Covid levels. People are attending events, artists are releasing music, and weekend schedules are saturated with club nights, warehouse parties, and festival announcements. Yet, local artists captured as little as 9 percent of New Zealand’s streaming, download, and physical sales revenue in 2024. In the official end-of-year Top 50 singles chart, exactly one New Zealand track was present, and only two nationwide commercial stations exceeded 20 percent local content. The scene is alive and economically potent, but it has become invisible. Without documentation, there is no one to report on the gig from last night, the new signing to an overseas label, or the specific reasons why a 34-year-old venue succumbed to a $692,000 debt. Invisible artists do not get streamed, booked, or paid; the 9 percent share is not a talent deficit, but a visibility crisis.

Propel has emerged to serve as the mirror the scene lost. This year, it has published more than 225 articles covering Aotearoa’s electronic music, including scene reports, artist features, and event coverage that once resided in the street press. This is not an attempt to replicate the output of a full newsroom, but a commitment to proving that dedicated coverage can exist again. The platform is built with the scene in mind, mapping over 2,700 global venues and hosting artist profiles that acknowledge the interconnectedness of the industry—where Auckland DJs play in Melbourne and Christchurch producers sign in Berlin. The goal is to transform the economics of the scene, not just describe them. By providing free tools—professional profiles, press kits, link-in-bios, and booking pages—Propel removes the gatekeeping once held by labels and agencies. The objective is a functional loop: coverage generates visibility, and visibility creates opportunity.

The work is far from finished. The next chapter involves ensuring this loop translates into tangible success for individual artists, through first bookings, first press hits, and enduring careers that trace their origins back to being seen here. This is a self-driven reconstruction. Because New Zealand lost its music media and no external entity is coming to rebuild it, the industry is building its own home. For artists, the platform offers free, rapid profile creation; for venue owners and promoters, it provides vital listings; and for the audience, the coverage remains free to read, ensuring the sound remains loud and, most importantly, heard.
