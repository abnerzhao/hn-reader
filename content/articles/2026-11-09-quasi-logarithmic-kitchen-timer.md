## medium

The creator found existing kitchen timers annoying and sought a better solution. While mechanical timers are imprecise and battery-operated models have mediocre reviews, the builder decided to construct a digital timer with an analog display and an adjustment knob. A key innovation was the use of a logarithmic scale to improve precision and legibility. The dial is divided into four sections with different ticking patterns: the first section uses ten-second increments, the second covers one to five minutes with minute and ten-second ticks, the third spans five to thirty minutes with five-minute and one-minute ticks, and the final section uses half-hour and ten-minute ticks. This design is self-documenting because labels like “1 min” and “30 min” indicate both the setting and the step size.

The display was created by repurposing a vintage Crompton 244-05VG-LS panel meter. Internally, the device functions as an ammeter with a full scale of 1 mA. The builder modified the panel and printed custom graphics on adhesive paper to create a custom face. An enclosure was designed and built from standard 4/4 maple stock, kept simple for easy cleaning. The enclosure was constructed using a bandsaw and assembled into a functional housing.

The circuit uses an 8-bit AVR microcontroller costing $1.50 to drive the meter via single-bit PWM on pin PC0. A small speaker driven through a variable duty cycle produces alarm sounds every 15 seconds. The encoder is a Bourns PEC16-2220F-N0024 that generates 24 pulses per revolution, providing tactile feedback. The entire device is powered by a compact 5 V wall wart.

The timer is activated by tapping an illuminated button and then dialing the desired time with a knurled knob. The wake-up tap prevents accidental activations, and the active period lasts 20 seconds with a beep and steady LED. The countdown begins three seconds after the last encoder input if the interval is greater than zero. While running, the interval can be adjusted seamlessly with the knob. Upon reaching zero, the device enters alarm mode, sounding every 15 seconds until the button is pressed again.

The creator notes that while modern hobby projects often rely on AI, this project was built by hand due to limited resources. ## hard

Like some of you, I have several kitchen appliances with integrated digital timers. I find them annoying: they are clunky to operate and if one uses them while cooking, the reflective surface gets covered in muck. Confusing, alien, evil technology. You can buy mechanical wind-up kitchen timers, but they’re imprecise and only ring once. There’s also a couple of cheap battery-operated units that can be adjusted by twisting an outer ring, but they have so-so reviews. But if there’s no good product to buy, why not make your own? I decided to build a digital timer with an old-school analog display and an easy-to-use adjustment knob. Furthermore, to improve precision and legibility, I figured it would be nice to go with a logarithmic scale. Well, sort of: a truly logarithmic display wouldn’t work well with customary time units. That said, I came up with a compromise solution that divides the dial into four linear sections. In the section up to the one-minute mark, major ticks represent ten-second increments. The next section covers 1 to 5 minutes, with major ticks every minute and minor ticks every ten seconds. The third section spans 5 to 30 minutes, with five-minute major ticks and one-minute minor ticks. Finally, for the range from 30 minutes to 3 hours, every major tick is half an hour and every minor tick is ten minutes. Although this sounds complicated, the scheme is self-documenting: the labels “1 min”, “5 min”, and “30 min” describe both the corresponding timer setting and the stepover in the section that follows. For example, three minutes is just “1 min” plus two major ticks, while 20 minutes is “5 min” plus three ticks. It doesn’t take long to get a hang of it.

For the actual electromechanical display, I repurposed a vintage, switchboard-style Crompton 244-05VG-LS panel meter. Internally, the device is an ammeter with a full scale of 1 mA. Analog meters of this sort have gone out of fashion but they’re still available from surplus outlets; I scored this pair on eBay for $30 each. To make a custom face for the meter, I simply disassembled the device, measured the dimensions, and printed replacement graphics on adhesive paper. With the display sorted out, I sketched out the design of an enclosure. I kept it uncomplicated to make the unit easy to clean. To make the enclosure, I grabbed standard 4/4 maple stock and resawed it on a bandsaw. The assembled enclosure is shown in the photo below. Let’s cook. Most of the effort was getting the UI right. The timer is activated by tapping an illuminated button on top and then dialing in the desired time with a knurled knob mounted on the side. The wake-up tap is meant to eliminate nuisance activations. The active period is 20 seconds, indicated by a beep and a steady LED. If there is no encoder input in that time window, the device goes back to sleep.

The countdown starts three seconds after the last encoder input, provided that the chosen interval is greater than zero. While the timer is running, the interval can be seamlessly adjusted with the knob. Once the countdown reaches zero, the device enters an alarm mode and starts making noise every 15 seconds until the button is pressed again. The alarm can be also canceled by turning the knob clockwise to add more time. Similarly to my earlier voltmeter clock project, the circuit uses an 8-bit AVR DB series microcontroller ($1.50) and drives the meter using single-bit PWM with a variable duty cycle on pin PC0. Square-wave audio is produced by a small speaker driven via pin PA6. An inexpensive 250 mA rail-to-rail op-amp, AD8531 ($1), is configured as a voltage follower to drive the speaker; many other chips with a reasonable current capacity — say, OPA310 ($0.80) — would also work fine. So would a two-transistor push-pull circuit. The speaker I used is probably CUI CMS-3048-058SP ($2.50). The encoder is Bourns PEC16-2220F-N0024; it produces 24 pulses per revolution and has detents that provide a nice tactile feel. The whole circuit is powered by a compact 5 V wall wart, CUI SWI5-5-NW-P5.
