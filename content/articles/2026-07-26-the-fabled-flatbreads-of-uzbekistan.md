## easy

In Uzbekistan, people love a special flatbread called non. Bakers use hot clay ovens called tandoors to bake it. This bread is golden, crunchy, and very delicious. You can find many kinds of non in different cities.

Bakers use metal stamps to make pretty patterns on the dough. This helps the bread cook evenly. People use non to celebrate weddings and big family parties. It is a symbol of friendship and hospitality.

In Uzbek culture, bread is very sacred. People do not use knives to cut it; they break it by hand. If bread falls on the ground, you must pick it up. You can place it on a tree for the birds.

## medium

Respect for non is respect for country. —Uzbek proverb The sun was coming up as I followed the scent of wood smoke and freshly baked bread down a chilly dirt lane on the outskirts of Tashkent. I weaved past bicycle‑mounted bread‑delivery men and bundled‑up children carrying steaming loaves in plastic bags, then slipped through an unmarked gateway in a mud‑plastered wall. In the courtyard stood fifth‑generation baker Raushanbek Ismailov, his upper body half‑inside a 315 °C tandoor. He slapped rounds of dough onto the white‑hot walls, pulling out dozens of fragrant, golden crusts called non (pronounced nahn) and laying them on an old metal bedframe to cool.

Raushanbek is a nonvoy—a bread baker who specializes in the Tashkent style. His day begins with an old cast‑iron tub of bubbling xamir, a sourdough prepared the night before. Handfuls of dough are weighed, rolled flat, stamped with traditional perforated designs using metal chekich, then given spoke‑like indentations with a bosma made from repurposed bicycle spokes. The flattened rounds are brushed with oil for a golden hue, sprinkled with sesame seeds, and coated with a mixture of powdered milk and water. On an average day he bakes about fifty large loaves and four hundred twenty‑five smaller ones. Tashkent‑style non is lighter, softer, more chewy and less dense than other regional varieties, and it reaches its peak of perfection for only a few hours, which is why bakeries employ fleets of bicycle delivery men who start service around five each morning to bring hot bread to cafés, stalls, markets, homes and restaurants.

At the Ulugbek non bazaar near Samarkand’s historic observatory, vendors pile their tables with non and festive patyr bread. For two hours Raushanbek and his assistant Gauhjaroy Kiyikiboyeva demonstrated the baking process. Samarkand patyr is made by folding multiple layers of dough with rendered mutton fat or butter, onion and herbs, then rolling, cutting and re‑rolling before baking, yielding a wide, flat, slightly flaky loaf reminiscent of puff pastry. Engagement bread, bright pink and yellow, is always bought in pairs because even numbers bring luck; at the ceremony the two families break and share it to formalize the union. On the wedding day the bride and groom each bite from two plain round loaves placed face‑to‑face; the loaves with the two bites missing are set aside until the next morning, when they finish them together as husband and wife. Before a son leaves for military service or study abroad, he likewise bites the edge of two facing loaves, which the family dries in the sun and hangs near the ceiling until his return.

In Bukhara I met Raim nonvoy, whose assistants offered me hot Bukhara non topped with fried eggs, cheese, yoghurt, honey and jam until I could hardly walk. When I asked why his bread cost more, he replied, “Because my bread tastes so much better than the others!” Bukhara non falls in density between the Tashkent and Samarkand styles and is sprinkled with blackened nigella seeds. The reverence for bread runs deep: Uzbek tradition holds that a loaf must never be cut with a knife; it is torn by hand, preserving the ancient gesture of “breaking bread.” When a piece falls to the ground it is picked up, placed on a wall or in a tree crook for the birds while saying “‘aysh Allah” (“God’s bread”), a practice echoed in the Spanish “Es pan de Dios.” At a Bukhara wedding the oldest and wisest woman holds two rounds of non on the bride’s head, walks her around the reception while reciting from the Qur’an, wipes her tears and leads her to the groom’s chamber.

Further northwest in Urgench, the capital of Khorezm Province, the local non is flatter and more cracker‑like, with chekich stamps covering most of its surface, leaving only a thin rim like pizza dough. Bakers decorate loaves with names from Uzbek folklore; at Salomat’s stall the names Tahir and Zuhra appear, a local Romeo and Juliet tale of star‑crossed lovers, fate, destiny and sacred love. Back in Tashkent I watched a small child drop a crust of non from a moving car; a group of women immediately retrieved it, blew on it, kissed it lightly, uttered a prayer and placed the crust on a tree branch for the birds, embodying the belief that bread is sacred and must never be allowed to lie waste.

## hard

The sun was rising as a scent of wood smoke and freshly baked bread drifted down a chilly dirt lane on the outskirts of Tashkent. Amidst the rush-hour traffic of bicycle-mounted delivery men and children carrying steaming stacks of bread in plastic bags, I entered a mud-plastered courtyard. There, fifth-generation baker Raushanbek Ismailov was working his upper body inside a 315-degree centigrade tandoor oven. Deftly, he placed rounds of dough onto a handheld cushion and slapped them against the white-hot walls. Minutes later, he emerged with dozens of fragrant, golden, crusty loaves called non, which he arranged on an old metal bedframe to cool. As a nonvoy, Raushanbek specializes in the Tashkent style, a variety of wheat-flour, leavened flatbreads characterized by the slightly smoky sourdough taste resulting from the clay tandoor.

Taking a bite of a warm, crisp, chewy loaf, the texture and roasted grain flavor immediately recalled my first experience with Central Asian tandoor bread in Herat, Afghanistan, in 1973. Now, forty-two years later, I found myself in Uzbekistan to discover why this bread is considered the finest in Central Asia. In the Ulugbek non bazaar of Samarkand, vendors pile tables with non and festive patyr bread. Observing Raushanbek and his assistant, Gauhjaroy Kiyikiboyeva, I watched the process: it begins with an old cast-iron bathtub of bubbling xamir, a sourdough prepared the night before. They weigh the dough, roll it flat, and apply traditional perforated designs using metal stamps called chekich, followed by spoke-like indentations. A brush of oil provides a golden color, finished with sesame seeds and a coating of powdered milk and water.

Tashkent-style non is uniquely lighter, softer, and more chewy than the denser varieties found in other parts of Uzbekistan. Because it reaches its peak of perfection only for a few hours, a fleet of bicycle delivery men and boys services cafés and homes starting at five o'clock each morning. While wheat and barley were domesticated in the Fertile Crescent roughly 10,000 years ago, leavened bread became essential following accidental discoveries in pharaonic Egypt. Whether through airborne yeast or the addition of Saccharomyces cerevisiae from the Nile, the tradition is ancient; Uzbek-style non is old enough to be mentioned in the Epic of Gilgamesh. Like coffee or tea, sharing bread is elemental to the region's hospitality and sacred traditions.

The non varies significantly by province. In Samarkand, I met Izzat nonvoy, who specializes in a Samarkand style that is darker, heavier, and more filling than the Tashkent variety, often coated in a light sheen of oil. At the Ulugbek non bazaar, vendors specialize in patyr, a festive, flaky bread made by folding dough with rendered mutton fat or butter, herbs, and onion. In the fabled Silk Road city of Bukhara, the bread falls between the density of the Tashkent and Samarkand styles and is often sprinkled with blackened nigella seeds. The philosopher Ibn Sina once claimed that eating a slice of obi non with raisins or walnuts in the morning would keep one full for a long time; in a Bukhara bakery, the tempting toppings of eggs, cheese, and honey made that claim feel quite plausible.

In Uzbekistan, non is a vessel for profound social and spiritual rituals. During engagement ceremonies, bright pink and yellow engagement bread is bought in pairs to signify good luck, and the families share it to formalize the union. At weddings, the oldest, wisest woman holds two rounds of non on the head of the bride as she says goodbye to her childhood home. In other instances, non is placed beneath the head of a newborn for a long life, or under the feet of a toddler to bless their journey. The bread is treated with immense sanctity; it is never cut with a knife but must be broken by hand. To drop a crumb is a sign of disrespect; if a piece falls, tradition dictates it be picked up and placed on a wall or a tree for the birds, accompanied by the words, 'God's bread.'

In the Urgench region of Khorezm Province, the non is flatter and more cracker-like, often decorated with names from Uzbek folklore, such as Tahir and Zuhra—the protagonists of a famous tale of star-crossed lovers. This connection between sustenance and storytelling highlights a culture where bread is more than mere food. Even in a passing moment on a Tashkent street, the sacredness remains visible: when a child accidentally drops a crust on the road, a woman immediately recovers it, kisses it, and places it on a tree branch for the birds, honoring the divine nature of the bread.
