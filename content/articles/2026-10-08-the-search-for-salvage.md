## easy

I heard a strange song in 2011. It was a stadium indie rock track called Salvage. The two songs were titled Electric and Searchlights. They sounded hard but easy.

The band name was generic. Search engines thought I wanted information about salvage companies. The original disc was lost. Many other groups share the name Salvage.

I tried many ways to find the group. A computer helped search old databases. Finally a record appeared on the official charts in 2006. It showed the label Key Production in London.

The producer was Greg Haver. He also worked with the Manic Street Preachers. He told me the band changed their name to OK Tokyo. Later he met the singer Sam Tattersall.

## medium

: In 2011 I heard a piece of music that made no sense to me. It was a perfect stadium‑indie‑rock track – thoughtful, expansive, layered yet simple – called “Electric” and its companion “Searchlights” by a band named Salvage. For the next fifteen years those two songs haunted me; they sounded professionally produced, yet I could find no trace of the group beyond the CD I had bought. The band name and track titles were generic, so search engines kept returning results for salvage companies, and the physical disc was long gone, leaving only a low‑resolution cover art and the knowledge that at least three other groups shared the name Salvage.

: I turned every new wave of frontier AI models at the mystery, treating them as both thought partners and search‑assistants, and logged each attempt in a markdown file. The AIs helped me locate the disc on Discogs, where a pending submission revealed no credits, matrix or SID codes, and they upscaled the sleeve art. From the Official Charts entry I learned the single had peaked at number 47 on the Independent Singles Chart for the week of 4–10 June 2006, giving me an artist ID and confirming a real release. Tracing the GS1 barcode led to Key Production in London, the manufacturer that shipped the disc, but the label “Despatches” had no company registration and no other catalogued releases.

: A Pandora search – a service I had overlooked because it is no longer major – finally returned the track, jogging my memory of where I first heard it and allowing me to retrieve the original purchase invoice from my email. I contacted the seller, but the address was outdated. Around the same time I discovered other people hunting for Salvage, and the AIs suggested that if the recordings had been a test, fragments of the lyrics might survive in other songs. I transcribed the lyrics and fed them back into the search, still with no match, until the AIs tried combining the band name with possible producer credits. A misspelled entry (“Searchlight” instead of “Searchlights”) led to Greg Haver, a known producer who had worked with the Manic Street Preachers.

: I emailed Greg, who replied quickly and put me in touch with Sam Tattersall, the singer and guitarist of Salvage. Greg also shared that shortly after the 2006 release the band changed its name to OK Tokyo, which explained why my earlier searches had come up empty. According to Sam, the group had originally aimed for big anthemic music, recorded early demos in Atlanta with a producer who did not grasp their vision, and later connected with Greg in Cardiff. The sessions at the Manic Street Preachers’ FASTER studios on a vintage Trident desk produced the “big epic” sound they wanted, with double‑tracked guitars, timed delays and reverb, and mastering by Shawn Joseph at Optimum. The band’s lineup was Sam Tattersall (vocals, guitars), Jon Tattersall (bass) and Matt Salvage (drums), all songs written by the group.

: Greg provided high‑resolution images of the CD slipcase, confirming the artwork and putting an end to the hunt. I can now hear “Electric” and “Searchlights” on YouTube or Apple Music, and anyone interested in the members’ later work can explore OK Tokyo on YouTube or Spotify, or the post‑band projects Glacier and Summit on the same platforms. Huge thanks go to Greg Haver and Sam Tattersall for reconnecting the story and adding the colour that finally solved the mystery.

## hard

:paragraph

In 2011 I heard a piece of music that made no sense to me. It was a polished stadium‑indie rock track – thoughtful yet expansive, layered but elegantly simple. The band was called Salvage and the two songs on the CD I bought were titled “Electric” and “Searchlights”. For the next fifteen years those tracks remained a maddening contradiction: they were clearly produced by someone with skill, yet I could find no trace of who made them, where they went, or why they were so elusive. I wondered whether they were a side‑project of a session band, a producer’s experiment, or something else entirely. Every few years I launched a new search, convinced that an answer existed and that I was simply missing a trick. Given my profession I expected to be adept at online sleuthing, but the quest proved frustratingly difficult.

:paragraph

The band name and track titles are generic, so search engines constantly redirected me to salvage companies, the far more likely topic. I had long ago lost the physical disc, removing any possible clues from the back or the disc itself; only a low‑resolution scan of the cover art survived. At least three other groups share the name Salvage – a German heavy‑metal act, a Brazilian math/post‑rock outfit, and a Dutch hardcore band. I could not recall exactly when or where I obtained the CD. Wherever the music appears online it is often listed as “Salvage Salvage” because the cover repeats the title. The imprint on the disc, Despatches, is itself a tiny and generic label, offering no further leads.

:paragraph

After a couple of years of dead ends I turned to frontier AI models, using them both as a thought partner and for their search‑tool proficiency. I assembled a prompt and dataset containing everything I knew – the cover art, audio files, metadata, and every URL I had uncovered – and instructed the model to keep a running log in an MD file, recording each attempt, what failed, and open hypotheses. I then downloaded that log, switched to a second model with the same background data, and asked it to re‑check the latest results, suggest new inputs, and push back on any assumptions. I already suspected the disc was listed on Discogs, so we examined the label page there and found a pending submission. The Discogs entry held no credits, no matrix or SID codes. The AI cropped and upscaled the sleeve image, but still revealed no label copy, catalogue number, URL, or credits. Both models proposed fresh approaches we had not tried before. For items the AI could not access – such as the Wayback Machine – I acted as the meat proxy, retrieving what was needed.

:paragraph

From the AI‑generated leads we located an Official Charts entry that confirmed the release was real: it peaked at number 47 on the Independent Singles Chart for the week of 4–10 June 2006. This gave us the Official Charts artist ID and a single chart record, which in turn made the absence of any other documentation even more puzzling. Using the barcode from the disc we traced it via GS1 to Key Production in London, the manufacturer that had shipped the CDs. I emailed Key Production, but unsurprisingly received no reply. We then searched Companies House and Discogs for the label “Despatches”. The Discogs page showed no company registration and no other releases, though we did find one other act credited to the label. An old Wikipedia mirror listed a band on Despatches Records during the relevant years, but following that trail led only to a dead end.

:paragraph

Returning to the digital data we had, we computed the CDDB disc ID and verified that legacy look‑ups no longer function on gnudb. An AcoustID fingerprint search returned no match. We checked the usual music services – Spotify, Bandcamp, Last.fm, AllMusic, MusicBrainz, eBay – all without success. The AI suggested that if the tracks had been a professional test, fragments of the lyrics might survive in other songs, so I transcribed the lyrics myself and fed them back for aggressive searching; again nothing turned up. I also searched SoundExchange’s ISRC database and the four unclaimed‑royalty lists, all to no avail. On a whim I tried Pandora, a service I had overlooked because it is no longer a major platform, and there the track appeared. Seeing it on Pandora jogged my memory: that was where I first heard the song, and it reminded me why I had bought the CD in the first place. I retrieved the original purchase invoice from my email, confirming the exact date, and emailed the seller, but the address was long inactive, yielding another dead end. While digging I discovered that several other people were hunting for the same band, which was oddly reassuring. I proposed that we combine the band name with possible collaborators – perhaps a member had listed the work on a credits page – so the AI ran searches against options such as “producer”. That finally broke the impasse.
