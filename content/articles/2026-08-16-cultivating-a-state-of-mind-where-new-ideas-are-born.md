## medium

In the early 2010s, the startup world was obsessed with coworking spaces and shared living. The idea was that founders needed a constant social scene of peers to help ideas percolate. However, Sam Altman, former president of Y Combinator, argued that this social proximity might actually be counterproductive. Great ideas are fragile, often sounding bad or nonsensical in their larval stages. When you are surrounded by others, the fear of being laughed at or being the outcast in a group acts as a band-pass filter, killing off the most original ideas in favor of something merely plausible.

This insight resonates with the lives of great artists and thinkers who emphasize the necessity of solitude. For them, being alone is not just a physical state but a mental one—a way to cultivate a sensitivity that allows them to hear the vague, emerging questions that others might overlook. The mathematician Alexander Grothendieck exemplified this through a profound period of isolation during his late teens. While his peers were rapidly mastering established mathematical frameworks within prestigious institutions, Grothendieck spent years in a local university, painstakingly reinventing concepts like the Lebesgue integral, unaware that the world already knew them.

This period of perceived 'wasted time' was actually essential. By working in isolation, Grothendieck developed the ability to observe his own thought processes and resisted the impulse to follow fashionable or established academic trends. He learned how to be alone, which gave him the confidence to develop his own mental frameworks rather than being a mere prisoner of the existing milieu. This capacity to linger in confusion and to be curious about the baffling rather than seeking the safety of known answers is what allowed him to surface truly transformative questions.

However, creativity requires a delicate balance between this solitude and social integration. The challenge is to interface with collaborators and mentors without losing the undiluted essence of one's personal curiosity. Once an individual enters the social world, they risk being swayed by the standards and norms of the community. The goal is to use the social world to further one's own aims rather than being controlled by them. Success brings mounting social expectations that make the creative state even more fragile, requiring constant protection to ensure that new ideas are not stifled by the demand for conformity.

## hard

In the early 2010s, a trend emerged to provide coworking spaces and shared living arrangements for startup founders, aiming to foster a thriving social scene where ideas could percolate among peers. However, countless attempts by startup incubators failed to produce any famous success stories. Sam Altman, formerly the president of Y Combinator, suggested that the absence of such communal spaces is precisely what enables their success. He argued that great ideas are inherently fragile and easy to kill in their larval stage. When innovators are surrounded by peers, the social pressure to avoid being the person picked last at recess can lead them to abandon brilliant but unconventional ideas in favor of something merely plausible. While coworking spaces may filter out the worst ideas, they act as a dangerous band-pass filter that simultaneously kills the best ones.

This necessity for solitude has been echoed by giants of creativity, from Pablo Picasso to James Baldwin. They suggest that serious work requires a deliberate cultivation of the state of being alone—not merely sitting in a room, but achieving a specific state of mind where the opinions of others no longer cause distress. In this state, one attains a heightened sensitivity to the vague, emerging questions and nascent ideas that reside within. This is not a simple matter of physical isolation, but a psychological preparation for the unspeakable labors that precede any major discovery. To understand this more deeply, one must look at the private working notes of individuals like the mathematician Alexander Grothendieck and the director Ingmar Bergman, who used such records to track their attempts to invite new ideas into the world.

Alexander Grothendieck’s approach was characterized by a profound dissatisfaction with the mechanical aspects of mathematics. He observed a tendency in the community to favor the 'hammer and chisel' of finished, polished theorems over the messy, dreamlike processes that actually generate them. Grothendieck sought to document the entire creative arc, including the mistakes, the confusion, and the sudden leaps forward. His monumental work, Récoltes et Semailles, emerged from twenty-nine months of probing his own psychology and the nature of the creative act. It is a work of intense force, seething with a sense of being misunderstood, yet it remains a profound portrait of the conditions required to reach out toward the unknown.

Grothendieck's unique ability stemmed from a period of intense intellectual isolation between the ages of seventeen and twenty. While his peers in Paris were highly trained and capable of quickly absorbing familiar concepts, they often remained prisoners of the existing mathematical milieu, unable to break beyond the bounds of what was already known. Grothendieck, having spent the war years in an internment camp, lacked formal schooling and was forced to reconstruct fundamental concepts like measurement theory and the Lebesgue integral from scratch. Although his mentors viewed his redundant efforts as a waste of time, this period of solitary struggle allowed him to develop an enduring confidence and a capacity to inhabit the cognitive spaces where truly original ideas reside.

This capacity to linger in confusion is a hallmark of intensely creative individuals. While many possess the brilliance to answer established questions, the most original thinkers possess the ability to surface entirely new ones. They are willing to wait for a subtle, powerful question to arise from open attention rather than seeking the safety of a legible, conventional problem. This ability to observe one's own thought processes allows a person to recognize their genuine curiosity, preventing them from merely reacting to social impulses or fashionable trends. By developing this internal framework first, a person can later interface with the professional community without losing themselves to its standards or norms.

However, maintaining this creative state is a fragile endeavor, particularly as success increases and social expectations mount. Creative individuals often require rituals and narratives to protect their mental space from external interference. The workbooks of Ingmar Bergman illustrate this struggle; they are fluid, unstructured collections of thoughts that shift from frustration to monologue without warning. His notes reveal a man constantly reminding himself not to be intimidated by outer reality or the demands of commerce, striving instead to protect his 'laral' ideas. This defensive posture is essential for those who seek to act not based on what the world demands, but on the intrinsic requirements of their own vision.
