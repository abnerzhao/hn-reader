## easy

I ran 600 tests on a small planning model. It learns to guess what happens next. Its guesses were as good as perfect physics.

Splitting long jobs into short steps helped most. Success went from 22% to 80%. Extra searching did not help much.

A memory of past runs gave new goals. Adding a cost for big jumps raised success to 85%. Changed copies of old runs filled gaps.

One small fix helped most. Judge only the block, not the pusher. This raised success by 60 points. The model itself never changed.

## medium

There is a big debate about what a world model should be. Generative world models predict the future in pixels, but Yann LeCun and others argue that most pixels do not matter for deciding what to do. JEPA is the proposed alternative. It has an encoder that turns each frame into a compact embedding, a predictor that predicts the next embedding from the current one and an action, and a planner that tries many action sequences and picks the one that ends closest to the goal embedding. LeWM is a JEPA world model tested on Push-T, where a robot pushes a T-shaped block to a target. I ran 600 experiments on it.

First, I replaced LeWM’s predictor with the simulator’s perfect physics. On 250 long-horizon tasks with the goal 75 steps away, vanilla LeWM succeeded 78% of the time and the perfect-physics version succeeded 77%. Along the paths it was trained on, LeWM’s predictions were already as good as perfect physics for planning. Vanilla LeWM succeeded only 22% on those long-horizon tasks, while exact physics reached 42%. Without retraining, I found four planning methods. Planning through subgoals every 25 steps raised success to about 80–85%, close to a planner given expert subgoals. LeWM’s useful planning horizon is about 25 steps, so chaining short searches works better than one long search.

A latent memory graph built from about 460,000 embedded demonstration states also worked. Each state linked to later states in its own demonstration and to nearby states in other demonstrations. Embedding distance ranked reachable subgoals as closer 87% of the time. Adding a cost to jumps between demonstrations raised success from 62% to 85%. The standard Push-T test is easier than it looks: every demonstration ends in about the same place, so many test goals sit on paths the demonstrations already travel. On 250 fresh episodes, LeWM succeeded only 44%, and changing the planner’s random seed flipped 38 episodes. I built a harder test where each task starts from one demonstration and takes its goal from another. The memory-graph planner succeeded 75% on standard tasks and 45% on the harder ones.

Transformed memories filled gaps in the graph. Deleting states near the goal cut success from 93% to 32%; shifting, rotating or mirroring demonstration segments brought it back to 85%. On the harder test, transformed segments raised success from 45% to 56%, and helped most when the goal lay against the flow of the demonstrations: from 27% to 46%. Goal scoring also mattered. With exact physics, scoring the pusher’s position as well as the block’s gave 9 successes out of 50, while scoring only the block gave 39. The goal image shows where the expert’s pusher happened to be, so the planner tries to match it. Comparing predictions against 16 versions of the goal with the pusher moved doubled LeWM’s success on the harder test, from 11% to 23%. A good goal should accept every end state that solves the task.

None of these discoveries changed the model’s weights. They apply to LeWM as published and should carry over to larger JEPA world models that plan the same way, at no extra training cost. The biggest open problem is generalizing to tasks without perfect demonstrations. The best planner holds the block on target 56% of the time on the harder test, and most remaining failures are goals that run against the direction of the data. Going in, I assumed LeWM’s predictions would be the bottleneck, but a JEPA small enough to train in a few hours already predicts about as well as the simulator. The next gap is choosing its own subgoals where no demonstration has been.

## hard

There is a live argument in AI about what a world model should be. The dominant breed is generative: it predicts the future by producing it, usually in pixels. Yann LeCun is among those who call this wasted effort, since most pixels bear no weight on the choice of action and many cannot be predicted at all. The rival proposal is JEPA, the joint-embedding predictive architecture, which runs an encoder, a predictor and a planner entirely in embedding space. The encoder compresses each observation frame into a learned vector describing the scene; the predictor takes that vector plus an action and forecasts the next frame's vector; the planner then simulates hundreds of action sequences inside the predictor and keeps the one whose predicted end state sits closest to the embedding of the goal. Only the features that matter for deciding are learned, and everything irrelevant is never represented at all.

LeWM, released this year by a team that includes LeCun, is such a model, and its benchmark is Push-T, a simulation in which a robot nudges a gray T-shaped block across a table until it occupies the green position painted in a goal image. I spent recent months running 600 experiments on it, and the first question was whether its predictions are the limiting factor. I replaced the learned predictor with the simulator's perfect physics and left everything else untouched, then tested both on 250 long-horizon tasks whose goals lay 75 steps away. Vanilla LeWM succeeded 78% of the time; the version with true physics succeeded 77%. Along the trajectories it was trained on, the model already plans as well as the simulator itself, so further gains must come from how the trained architecture is used, not from its weights.

The first discovery concerns horizon. As published, LeWM reaches only 22% on tasks with a goal 75 steps away, yet it hits 89.7% at 25 steps, within a point of the exact-physics planner at 90.5%. The familiar explanation — that small errors compound across a long plan — would demand a better model, but the arithmetic points elsewhere: giving exact physics all 75 steps at once yields 42%, while splitting the same task into 25-step segments and re-planning at each subgoal yields 80%. Chaining short searches inside the model's accurate range is hierarchical planning, and it is worth roughly three times what perfect physics buys. Extra search cannot substitute for it: running the planner four times and keeping the lowest-cost plan took four times the compute and added less than one point, because the runs failed together.

The second discovery removes the need for expert subgoals. LeWM's encoder embeds every frame of its training demonstrations, some 460,000 states, which I connected into a graph: each state links to its successors in the same demonstration and to nearby states in others. The planner searches that graph for a path from the current state to the goal, and this works because embedding distance encodes physical reachability — a subgoal reachable within 25 steps is ranked closer 87% of the time. The first version failed in an instructive way: free jumps between demonstrations were strung into physically impossible routes, with 318 of 1,015 plans using six or more jumps. Charging a cost for every cross-demonstration jump lifted success on the 250 tasks from 62% to 85%, matching a planner handed ideal expert subgoals.

That match deserves scrutiny, because the standard test is easier than it looks: its goals sit along the very paths demonstrations travel, and for 84% of them the graph already contained a nearly identical state from another demonstration. The official 50 episodes also flatter the model, which scored 68% there but only 44% on 250 fresh episodes drawn the same way, with the planner's random seed alone flipping 38 episodes. I therefore built a harder test that takes each start from one demonstration and each goal from another, counting success only if the block holds target for the final 10 steps; under those rules the memory-graph planner scores 75% on standard tasks and 45% on the new ones.

The remaining two discoveries attack that gap. First, memory can be stretched by transformation: pushing physics is invariant to shifting, rotating or mirroring the table, so when the graph has nothing near a goal, demonstration segments from elsewhere can be transformed to fill the hole. Deleting all states near the goal had collapsed success from 93% to 32%; transformed copies restored it to 85%, and a graph built from 1% of the demonstrations plus transforms matched one built from 10%. On the harder test they raise success from 45% to 56%, helping most where data is thinnest: goals lying against the flow of demonstrations rise from 27% to 46%.

Second, the goal itself was scoring the wrong thing. An interactive demo exposed this: with the pusher placed just behind the target in the goal image, the robot reached within 14 pixels, then pushed the block away while chasing the pusher's pictured position. The effect is not a quirk of prediction, because an exact-physics planner given the same 50 tasks succeeded 39 times when the cost covered the block alone and only 9 times when it also covered the pusher — 30 flips in one direction, none the other, an imbalance chance would produce twice in a billion. LeWM cannot simply ignore the pusher, since its goal embedding fuses block and pusher with no label separating them; searching instead over 16 copies of the goal, each with the pusher at a position seen in demonstrations, doubled success on the harder test from 11% to 23%.

None of this retrains anything: four planning changes applied to LeWM as published, each likely to carry over to larger JEPA models that plan the same way. The deeper lesson is that a goal should be a set of valid end states rather than one photographed frame, and large demonstration sets already contain many such frames. What remains open is generalization beyond them — the best planner still holds target on only 56% of the harder tasks, mostly where goals run against the data — which requires a higher level that proposes subgoals no demonstration contains, something today's learned hierarchies such as H-JEPA do not yet deliver beyond flat LeWM. I assumed the predictions would be the bottleneck; they were not. Choosing subgoals where no demonstration has been is what I am working on now.
