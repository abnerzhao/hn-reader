## easy

A critic named Ed Zitron often says big tech companies like Meta and Google are dying. He claims they are desperate and pushing AI because they cannot grow. But public numbers show these companies had large increases in both money made and profit. For example, Meta's profit grew by 48% in 2024. Google's profit grew by 33%.

Zitron also predicted that AI technology had peaked. He said in 2024 that it could not improve further. He claimed companies like OpenAI would fail. These predictions were wrong. AI models became much stronger. OpenAI's revenue grew faster than he said it would.

He made many other claims that were incorrect. He said a data service called Cursor would die.

## medium

To assess the accuracy of prominent AI skeptic Ed Zitron's predictions, an analysis was conducted, revealing a consistent pattern of error. The author of the analysis maintains a neutral stance on AI progress, having previously critiqued both overly optimistic and pessimistic forecasts. The examination focused on Zitron's claims that major tech companies like Meta, Google, and Microsoft were dying and thrashing due to a lack of growth ideas, resorting to AI out of desperation. Financial data contradicts this, showing substantial revenue and profit growth for all three companies from 2023 through the first half of 2026, suggesting their ecosystems are not in decline. Zitron's reasoning was found to rely on minor, unsupported issues, such as using inaccurate third-party data to claim a decline in Facebook's monthly active users, while ignoring the companies' strong overall financial health.

The analysis argues that Zitron's method involves cherry-picking negative details to support a broader, incorrect narrative. For instance, he focused on a specific Google executive's alleged damage to search quality, but this does not account for growth in other major divisions like YouTube and Google Cloud. The core of his argument—that AI is a desperate move by stagnant companies—is undermined by the companies' robust financial performance. Furthermore, his predictions about AI's capabilities have been repeatedly wrong. He has consistently claimed that generative AI has peaked, is a dead-end, and cannot improve, yet models have continued to advance significantly in power and efficiency throughout 2024 and 2025. His forecasts for company performance, such as OpenAI's revenue and Anthropic's valuation, have also proven to be far too pessimistic.

A review of numerous specific predictions from February 2024 to November 2025 shows a long list of failures. Zitron repeatedly stated that AI progress was stalling, that models wouldn't get better, and that companies like OpenAI and Anthropic were on the brink of collapse. In reality, AI capabilities advanced substantially, and these companies experienced strong growth. He also incorrectly predicted the failure of other AI-related ventures like Cursor and CoreWeave, which have since succeeded. While some statements were vague enough to be unfalsifiable, the track record of falsifiable claims is overwhelmingly negative. The analysis suggests that Zitron's credibility suffers not just from wrong predictions, but from flawed reasoning, as his cited numbers often don't support his conclusions, and his arguments ignore critical context about the industries he critiques.

## hard

I was curious how well the predictions of the most widely cited AI skeptic Ed Zitron have performed, so I examined his claims directly. My own position on artificial intelligence has remained neutral, neither strongly pro nor anti, and this impartiality shapes how I evaluate his assessments. In 2022 I reviewed futurist predictions including those of Kurzweil and found them generally inaccurate regarding both outcomes and reasoning. Earlier in 2015 I wrote about widespread underestimation of AI's capacity to displace workers, noting that many experts had repeatedly stated this trend. Zitron's approach follows a similar pattern of claiming impossibility based on current limitations, asserting that those who declare certain outcomes unachievable are likely mistaken. This stance reflects a broader skepticism toward rapid technological advancement, though it lacks concrete evidence beyond rhetorical framing.

One prominent example involves Zitron's 2024 assertion that major technology companies including Meta, Google, and Microsoft are dying and desperately seeking ways to reignite growth through artificial intelligence. He presented revenue and profit figures suggesting declining performance, but these numbers derive from third-party sources rather than official reports. Meta's reported revenue grew from $135 billion in 2023 to $165 billion in 2024 according to similarweb data, while Google's revenue increased from $307 billion to $350 billion during the same period. Even allowing for these improvements, the notion that these companies are dying contradicts observable market behavior and fundamental business logic. Zitron's reasoning fails to account for diversified product lines that sustain growth independent of search or advertising dominance.

Another instance concerns Google's search quality and the supposed influence of Prabhakar Raghavan on search degradation. Zitron criticized Raghavan as a traitor to computer science whose actions damaged Google search, yet he offered no verifiable evidence linking Raghavan's decisions to measurable declines in search relevance. The claim that Google faces existential threats from search issues ignores the existence of robust alternatives like YouTube and cloud services that generate substantial revenue streams. Similarly, Zitron's prediction that Meta is collapsing due to alleged drops in monthly active users relies on external metrics rather than internal financial indicators, rendering the argument circular and unsupported by solid analysis.

The pattern reveals a systematic flaw in Zitron's methodological approach. He frequently extracts isolated numerical details from complex datasets and presents them as definitive proof of larger trends, ignoring contextual factors that explain apparent contradictions. When discussing OpenAI's trajectory, Zitron projected revenues of $3.7 billion for 2024 and $11.6 billion for 2025, figures that exceed actual performance and demonstrate a fundamental misunderstanding of market dynamics. His assertion that OpenAI's growth is stalling ignores documented increases in computational resources, expanded partnerships, and successful product launches. These errors accumulate across multiple predictions, creating a cumulative distortion of reality that undermines any potential credibility.

Beyond individual miscalculations, Zitron's communication style prioritizes emotional resonance over analytical rigor. His writing employs aggressive language and personal attacks, which serve to mobilize readers already inclined toward skepticism rather than persuade undecided audiences. Commentators note that his economic analyses are fundamentally flawed and appear designed to mislead rather than inform. The reliance on anger-driven rhetoric creates a feedback loop where supporters amplify his claims while critics dismiss them as mere polemic. This strategy sacrifices intellectual honesty for engagement, resulting in a body of work that satisfies ideological preferences but fails to advance genuine understanding of technological development.

When examining how others cite Zitron, the effect becomes clear. By presenting himself as an authoritative figure who has scrutinized quantitative data, he lends unwarranted weight to his conclusions. Readers who encounter his name often assume his statistical references carry the force of peer-reviewed research, even when the underlying calculations contain deliberate errors. The disconnect between his claimed expertise and the actual coherence of his arguments exposes a troubling gap between public perception and scholarly standards. Ultimately, Zitron's predictions demonstrate a persistent inability to distinguish between meaningful signals and noise, leading to repeated failures that contradict observable technological trajectories.

This pattern extends throughout his entire career, where forward-looking declarations of AI stagnation coexist with retrospective claims that capabilities have remained static. Predictions that AI reached peaks in mid-2024 or that models have exhausted their learning potential ignore continuous improvements in architecture, training efficiency, and application breadth. The accumulation of such contradictory assertions suggests a fundamental misreading of technological momentum rather than a principled critique of current limitations. While occasional insights may emerge, the overwhelming record indicates that Zitron's assessments are unreliable and his reasoning insufficiently grounded in empirical reality.

The final assessment remains unequivocal: Zitron's predictions have consistently failed to materialize, and his methodological approach demonstrates a tendency toward selective presentation of data combined with rhetorical strategies that prioritize conviction over accuracy. His work serves primarily as a vehicle for reinforcing existing skepticism rather than providing constructive analysis of artificial intelligence's trajectory. Future evaluations should continue to monitor his claims with appropriate skepticism, recognizing that his historical pattern of error makes his current assertions unlikely to differ significantly from previous disappointments.
