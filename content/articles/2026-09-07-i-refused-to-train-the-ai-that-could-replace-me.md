## easy

A recruiter offered me a job training an AI to teach students and grade papers. The pay was very high for South Africa, where many people are unemployed. I agreed to an interview, even though the AI would learn to do my work one day.

The problem is common in Africa. Companies pay experts low wages to teach AI systems that later replace them. This happens because the continent has many educated workers but few good jobs. The AI jobs are different from simple data entry work. They need expert knowledge.

The AI would learn not just facts, but my professional judgment. It would learn how I teach, grade essays, and make fair decisions.

## medium

After finishing my Ph.D. on AI in South African agriculture, I expected to start an academic career. Instead, a recruiter offered me a job training an AI system to design courses, teach students, and grade essays—essentially transferring my entire expertise to a machine. I agreed to an interview, questioning my own motives. Why would I help build a technology that could replace me? The answer lies in South Africa’s harsh reality: the role paid 600 rand ($37) per hour, while the national minimum wage was just 30.23 rand ($2), and youth unemployment reached 47.4% in mid-2026. When your livelihood clashes with your principles, the choice is agonizing. I am not alone in this dilemma. Across the globe, people are hired to train the AI systems that will eventually take their jobs. Writers are paid low wages to “humanize” AI-generated text. In India, waste sorters and welders wear cameras to record their daily tasks for about 250 rupees ($2.60) an hour. This data trains humanoid robots to replace those very workers. Though our pay scales differ drastically, we are all doing the same thing: teaching machines human judgment. If this becomes the norm, Africa is especially vulnerable. The continent has one of the lowest AI adoption rates, with most countries below the global average of 18% among working-age adults. South Africa is slightly higher at 23%. Yet Africa has the world’s youngest population and a growing number of educated professionals facing high unemployment and low wages. For AI companies, this creates an incentive to extract expert knowledge from African professionals at lower costs than in the West. The jobs offered are more sophisticated than simple data labeling. I was scouted not just to transfer knowledge, but to teach the AI my judgment—my ability to decide what concepts matter in a course, how to evaluate essays, and why one student deserves 75% over another. The AI would learn how I make decisions, not just what I know. What does it mean to hand over the judgment that defines our professions to a machine? Many skilled fields—law, medicine, teaching—rely on discretion and context, which were once thought impossible to automate. This is why doctors, lawyers, and engineers are now being recruited by AI platforms. In my own experience, no human was involved in the hiring process. An AI interviewed me for 45 minutes and then emailed feedback on my strengths and weaknesses. This used to be a human decision, but now AI decides who gets hired. The AI even suggested I retake part of the assessment. I didn’t, and I ended the process. I wish I could say my refusal was purely principled—that I feared helping build a tool that might replace my work. But I still don’t fully understand my discomfort. What kind of judgment are we teaching these systems? Are we teaching them to be fair, ethical, and truly understand context? I still don’t know why I walked away. Perhaps I will understand in time. For now, I continue searching for an academic job, one where I can teach students, not AI.

## hard

After completing a doctoral dissertation on artificial intelligence applications in South African agriculture earlier this year, I anticipated embarking on an academic trajectory involving pedagogy, scholarly supervision, and mentorship of emerging researchers. My initial expectation proved misplaced when a recruiter approached me with an unprecedented proposition: to train an artificial intelligence system capable of designing assessments, instructing undergraduate cohorts, and grading essays. The compensation package—600 rand per hour—appeared extraordinary given that the national minimum wage in South Africa stands at 30.23 rand, while youth unemployment reached 47.4 percent in the second quarter of 2026. This stark disparity forced me to confront a profound ethical quandary: how might one reconcile the pursuit of livelihood with the conviction to assist a technological entity poised to supplant future employment.

The socioeconomic landscape of South Africa rendered the opportunity compelling yet morally fraught. With wages so low relative to the value of specialized knowledge, the prospect of earning a sustainable income through direct engagement with artificial intelligence development seemed irresistible. Yet the question persisted—what compels an individual to relinquish expertise to a machine that promises to replicate their cognitive functions? The tension between material necessity and intellectual integrity became impossible to ignore, prompting me to examine whether such a compromise aligns with my fundamental values.

Widely reported trends suggest a broader phenomenon wherein individuals are increasingly recruited to train the very artificial intelligences destined to displace their occupations. Skilled writers are compensated minimally to humanize outputs generated by algorithms, while in India waste collectors and welders strap smartphones or cameras to their heads for mere 250 rupees per hour to document daily labor, providing raw material for robot training programs. These instances reveal a disturbing pattern: the transfer of human cognition and experience to machines, regardless of economic disparity.

Africa occupies a precarious position within the nascent artificial intelligence ecosystem. Despite possessing some of the world’s lowest adoption rates—below the global working‑age average of approximately eighteen percent—the continent hosts one of the youngest populations and the largest concentration of highly educated professionals operating amid pervasive unemployment, depressed wages, and modest economic outlooks. Consequently, artificial intelligence firms perceive substantial financial incentives in extracting expert insight from African talent at substantially reduced cost compared to Western markets.

The demands of contemporary artificial intelligence roles differ markedly from traditional data‑labeling positions historically outsourced to Kenya, Nigeria, and other regions. My recruitment targeted not merely factual recollection but the nuanced judgment cultivated through years of classroom instruction and undergraduate teaching. Over countless instructional hours I developed the capacity to discern substantive conceptual importance, evaluate argumentative coherence, distinguish authentic comprehension from rote memorization, and assign appropriate grades reflecting genuine mastery—a form of pedagogical discernment that transcends simple information retrieval.

If an algorithm were to absorb both the repository of knowledge and the underlying decision‑making processes that produced it, the outcome would transcend mere data assimilation. The system would internalize not only what I knew but also the heuristics and criteria that guided my evaluations, effectively encoding the architecture of human judgment itself. Such a transformation raises unsettling implications regarding the delegation of moral agency to computational architectures.

Professional domains steeped in discretion—such as law, medicine, and education—have traditionally resisted automation due to their reliance on contextual interpretation rather than rule‑based application. Currently, leading artificial intelligence platforms including Outlier, Mercor, and Surge actively recruit practitioners whose expertise lies precisely in exercising legal, clinical, and pedagogical judgment. During my own selection process, no human evaluator participated; an artificial intelligence conducted a forty‑five minute interview, subsequently delivering feedback on my strengths and deficiencies. This shift marks a decisive moment in which algorithmic assessment supersedes human deliberation in determining employability.

My refusal stemmed primarily from principled concern that contributing to the creation of a technology likely to eclipse my profession would constitute complicity in my own obsolescence.
