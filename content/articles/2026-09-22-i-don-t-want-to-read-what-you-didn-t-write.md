## easy

I see people using AI to write plans and reports. But these texts are often hard to read. They have too much detail but no clear meaning.

For example, AI-written design documents are just summaries. They don't explain why things are done. I feel confused when I read them. A survey shows most people stop reading AI-written articles.

I use AI to check my own writing. It helps with facts, grammar, and citations. But I write every word myself. AI is good for small tasks, not for creating content.

In the end, I want to read human writing. It has voice and feeling. AI texts are dry and lifeless. Writing should come from the person, not a machine.

## medium

I am tired of reading text created by artificial intelligence. Many people now produce large amounts of documents, including design proposals, business plans, and reports, using AI tools. These texts are often incomprehensible and feel unnatural. While AI helps me write faster, I am frustrated by the constant stream of AI-generated material. In this essay I explain why I dislike these writings and show how I find value in AI for certain tasks.

A common pattern involves creating something new with AI and then having the machine summarize it into a design document later. Such documents are difficult to read and lack human context. They are simply summaries written by a machine in exhaustive detail without perspective. The authors of these documents grow impatient when people are not engaged, and it is hard to stay interested because the work is already finished. Similarly, pull request summaries written by machines contain many details about changes and merges, but they offer no real value. I question why we do this and wonder what the purpose is.

Personal messages written by AI also fail to convey genuine emotion. Once I asked someone to write a personal message about a sensitive topic after careful AI workshops, the result felt impersonal and disjointed. It contained all the necessary parts but lacked coherence as a whole. I would prefer someone to write with their own voice and personality, even if they struggle to find the perfect words. Communication with AI lacks vulnerability and risk, which are essential for true connection.

Research shows that most readers stop reading articles they suspect are AI-written. Seventy-eight percent avoided such articles, and seventy-one percent refused to trust the author in the future. Most readers strongly prefer human-written content despite its flaws. The authentic voice creates stronger connections with people. Bjarne Stroustrup noted that hearing an author's unique voice makes reading satisfying. Without this personal touch, writing becomes dry and academic.

AI can assist with verification and organization. During my academic paper writing, I used AI to check facts against source code and configuration files. It helped me catch errors and improve clarity. AI excels at summarizing and presenting information efficiently. However, AI is not capable of true creative writing. It lacks empathy and a deep understanding of human readers. Models optimize for predicting the next word statistically, not for emotional resonance.

Future improvements may allow AI to write better. Standards like ASD-STE100 exist for technical documentation, and tools like Pangram can detect AI-generated text. Yet some forms of writing, such as poetry and literature, resist simple translation into language. Narrative requires attention to detail and cannot be easily summarized. I do not want to live in a world where AI summarizes important things into unreadable text. I want to hear humans, with all their imperfections. I want their interpretations of beauty and relationships. My intention is to seek writing that cuts through to reveal what truly matters.

## hard

People who have rarely produced original writing are now generating long design proposals, business plans, documentation, presentations, tickets, pull requests, blog posts, and meeting summaries with AI. Much of it is unreadable. AI helps me write better and faster, but I am tired of reading text that feels as if no one chose it, shaped it, or stood behind it. A common pattern is especially frustrating: someone uses AI to build something, then uses AI again to turn what has already been built into a retrospective design document. Reading such a document is not merely difficult; it is punishing. A design document should build consensus, bring people along, and refine ideas through slow, deliberate thought. Instead, it becomes a machine-made summary, exhaustive in detail yet empty of context and perspective. The people who produce these documents are often impatient when others are not engaged, but that is hard to avoid, since the thing they built may already work. Pull-request summaries are much the same: rich in detail about what changed, what was split, what was merged, what remained untouched, and what tests were added, yet silent on why the work matters, how risky or urgent it is, where input is needed, and what deserves attention. AI-made tickets and meeting summaries often share this quality: statements placed outside the circumstances that would make them meaningful.

The problem becomes more personal when AI is used to manage human relationships. I once received a message about a sensitive matter that was clearly shaped with AI, apparently to add nuance and avoid offence. The result was impersonal, dispassionate, and disjointed. It contained all the expected parts, but it did not make sense as a whole, and I had no desire either to finish engaging with it or to answer. I would much rather receive someone’s own voice, even awkwardly expressed, incomplete, passionate, or risky, than a careful arrangement with the vulnerability removed. In relationships, vulnerability and risk are not defects; they are part of the process. If attempts to remove them succeed, the relationship itself is removed with them. Simon Sarris’s “Resist Summary” keeps returning to this point: one should write as much as possible from one’s own empiricism and senses, giving readers one’s own characterization of life or events. Perhaps the most offensive use I have seen was someone using AI to summarize my comments on a proposal and then presenting that summary as a response to me.

Cynthia Dunlop recently shared survey results on reading AI-written blog posts, and the findings are severe. When readers believe a post is AI-assisted or AI-authored, most stop reading, and many avoid the person in future. Bryan Cantrill described this reaction as a kind of LLM-triggered ejection handle: the brain pulls it mid-sentence in an act of self-preservation. The strongest result was the overwhelming preference for a person’s own writing, flaws and idiosyncrasies included, over a soulless AI rewrite. Authentic writing connects because it lets a human presence come through. Bjarne Stroustrup, the creator of C++, once said in a discussion about writing books that when he reads, he wants to hear the person’s voice in his head, with strange accents and peculiar speech patterns. If the text feels like dry academic prose, then it is merely another piece of dry academic prose. The same issue appears when AI output is shared with someone who did not create the prompts. The person who prompted the model already has context: the constraints, the artefacts, the source code, logs, statistics, images, and the unfolding exchange of call and answer. That person can skim, judge relevance, and notice error. Someone receiving the same text later has little context and must read exhaustively, line by line, peering into the machine’s internals in hopes of reconstructing meaning. It is only natural to stop.

AI can still be extraordinarily useful for writing, but the useful role is not the same as original composition. I recently wrote an academic paper and used AI extensively; it made the process faster and more enjoyable, yet AI did not write a single line of the paper. I wrote it in LaTeX and gave the system the journal style guide, the template, previous papers from the journal, the source code for the software systems I described, and the configuration, logs, and metrics from production deployments. After writing a paragraph, I would often ask AI to check whether my account was correct by consulting the source code, configuration, logs, or metrics. For example, it could verify whether I had described the indexed database columns correctly or how rows in Parquet files were sorted. This let me remain in the flow of writing while the previous paragraph was checked, and it helped me find omissions and inaccuracies. Reversing the process, asking AI to write the paragraph from the same context, was never valuable. It was consistently unpleasant to read and often wrong.

AI was also useful for keeping me in context. It completed citations: instead of stopping to look up a paper, website, or book, fill in the BibTeX format, and insert the reference, I could leave an instruction such as “reference the Amazon Aurora paper” and continue. It was ruthless at finding spelling and grammar mistakes, suggesting simplifications for sentences that were too long or unclear, and drawing technical diagrams in TikZ, which saved me from learning the syntax. It even found a subtle notation error that four expert human reviewers missed. In one paragraph I had used L1 when I meant L2, an error made harder to see because colleagues had to translate between level names and generation names in their heads. The model found it immediately. Yet the one thing AI was exceptionally good at writing was the abstract: the most terse, mechanical, and inhuman part of the paper, and therefore the most abstracted. I included it unchanged. That should not be surprising. AI is good at summary, abstraction, and re-presentation of original work.

It would be a mistake to assume AI can never improve as a writing instrument. Model development is still early, and many systems are trained to be factual, direct, and correct rather than creative. Still, Murat Demirbas has argued that the quality of LLM writing may have plateaued because, unlike programming, mathematics, or accounting, writing lacks a clear formulation and a verifiable output. LLMs do not possess an active mental model of a particular human reader; they optimize the statistical probability of the next word across a vast dataset. They cannot empathize with a reader because they have no lived human experience, and they have no stake in the consequences. Two efforts to improve the situation interest me. ASD-STE100 Simplified Technical English, developed from an aerospace working group in the 1980s, combines a controlled dictionary with grammar and style rules, and a version has been created for AI models. It is not suitable for all AI-generated text, but it may help with installation instructions, runbooks, and similar documentation. Pangram, a model fine-tuned to detect AI writing, responds to Bryan Cantrill’s claim that using an LLM to write voids the social contract between writer and reader: readers should not have to labour over a sentence that the writer did not labour to create. Cantrill has gone so far as to require public writing at Oxide to be reported by Pangram as human-authored, and such a tool may at least save us from the laziest and worst AI writing.

There are also things that cannot be reduced to direct, factual, concise language: poetry, literature, song, mythology, humour, theatre. Iain McGilchrist writes in “The Matter with Things” that the more important something is, the more we struggle to reduce it to language. Ask an LLM for a reliable formula for falling in love, and the answer will expose the problem. AI-generated detail can create the illusion of meaning, but mimicking knowledge, intelligence, and understanding is not the same as writing from lived experience. The whole cannot be decomposed into parts without substantial loss. Some things can be conveyed only indirectly, and to recover depth, or to say something genuinely new, we must attend to what is implicit. This is not limited to art. Business, engineering, science, operations, incident response, product development, performance engineering, security work, performance reviews, and mission statements all involve uncertainty and creativity. Narrative often matters as much as facts and conclusions. Good systems thinking, like good anthropology or psychology, is difficult to summarize because narrative resists compression, and the opposite of summary is attention to detail. The observer is part of the context, and writing something down can lose part of it. Systems work requires discovery, an openness to the unknown, and a willingness to be surprised. Normal language does not always have the words.

I do not want to live in a world where important things are summarized by AI into unreadable text and then deciphered by AI on the other side. I want to hear a person, imperfections included. I want an interpretation of aesthetics, beauty, quality, relationship, and time. I want to know what feels important, what cuts through, and what really matters. I do not want a summary of facts and connections severed from experience and relationship. McGilchrist’s point is that the way we attend to the world changes what comes into being, the only world we can know. If you ask a machine to do something for you, you risk asking not for what you want, but for what the machine does well. If it summarizes well and you rely on it, you may reshape your questions to receive better summaries. That looks like training a machine, but it also trains you to ask certain kinds of questions and omit others. Intentional writing will probably become more valuable. People who write in order to think, create, share, or capture something important without stating it outright will continue to produce original work. People who were never writers will use AI to produce large quantities of text. As that text multiplies, we may fail to notice what has been disrupted because we have already become shallower. The search for certainty limits our humanity. Sometimes it is more important not to find the words. I do not want to read what you did not write.
