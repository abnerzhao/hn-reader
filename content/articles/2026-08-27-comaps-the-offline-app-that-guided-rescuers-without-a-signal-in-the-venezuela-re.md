## easy

Anton used an offline app called CoMaps during the Venezuela earthquake. This app worked without internet or cell signals. It helped rescuers find their way in hard zones. The app is free and open source. It keeps the map saved on the phone.

The app uses OpenStreetMap data. Volunteers map buildings and roads everywhere. Once downloaded, the map stays active offline. It lets teams move and plan tasks easily. Rescue workers found it very useful.

The team hopes to improve updates soon. They want maps to change often, not just once a week. This helps people find safe paths fast. The goal is to save lives in future crises.

## medium

On the second day after the earthquake struck Venezuela, Anton made his way to what responders were already calling ground zero, the most devastated sector. Although he had been assisting in Caracas earlier, he now entered an area with no cellular coverage. He did not need to improvise because the offline mapping app CoMaps was already installed on his phone. He later recalled that the app proved indispensable as soon as he stepped into the signal‑free zone, allowing him to locate destinations that the command centre requested.

A few days later Anton joined a mixed rescue team that had arrived from El Salvador, including firefighters, Civil Protection units and other groups who were receiving instructions from a command centre back in their home country. Most of the teammates were unfamiliar with CoMaps; only one person possessed a map and was guiding the rest. Anton took on logistical duties, translating the centre’s move‑orders into concrete routes whenever the target appeared on the map. During this work he noticed that the team’s map coordinator was relying on a far more limited navigation app that lacked the detail contributed by a volunteer community and could not function fully offline. When Anton showed him CoMaps, the coordinator described the experience as a discovery he loved making.

CoMaps is built on OpenStreetMap data, the collaboratively edited map often likened to Wikipedia. The app is non‑commercial, fully open source and designed to work 100 % offline: once a region’s map is downloaded, it continues to operate using only the phone’s GPS, making it ideal for emergencies. For much of its first year a major drawback was the slow map‑update cycle – turning raw OpenStreetMap data into usable maps required a powerful server and an app store release, which together meant updates could take ten days. Months before the Venezuela response the CoMaps team decoupled map updates from app upgrades and upgraded their server, reducing processing time to about three days and enabling a fresh global map release each week.

The collaboration with the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap Team (HOT) had begun earlier in the year when Anton and Bastian presented CoMaps to HOT’s Geospatial Tech Working Group. Many of the technical improvements they had already made – such as the ability to share downloaded maps directly between devices – aligned with HOT’s vision even though they had not been requested. Emilio Mariscal of HOT later noted that CoMaps was already generating weekly maps of Venezuela when the earthquake hit, allowing responders to see the thousands of buildings volunteers had mapped through HOT’s Tasking Manager. He thanked Bastian for his rapid support.

The true value of CoMaps lies in its accessibility to non‑specialists. As Céline Jacquin, HOT’s Latin America Senior Manager, explained, the app lets organisations without the capacity to download and analyse raw OpenStreetMap data use the maps directly in the field. Bastian likens this to photography: the best camera is the one you already have in your pocket, because a sophisticated tool left at home is never used. Consequently, groups such as Techo and the Red Cross can now consult the latest mapping instantly, without needing training during a crisis.

Today the volunteer CoMaps team can prioritize an emergency update and have new maps ready for download in the field within roughly thirty‑five hours. Their ambition is to move from a weekly global refresh to region‑specific updates every twelve hours, so disaster‑affected communities can rely on maps that reflect the very latest volunteer contributions. For Anton and Bastian, knowing that their non‑commercial, humanitarian‑driven tool is becoming a trusted link between HOT’s OpenStreetMap work and those who need it on the ground is both a reward and a guiding goal. In the next emergency, wherever it occurs, a rescuer is likely to find his way again thanks to a map built building by building by volunteers who never met in person.

## hard

On the second day after the earthquake, Anton went down to what everyone had started calling ground zero, the hardest-hit area. He had been helping in Caracas before, but now he was heading into a zone with no cell coverage. He didn’t have to improvise because he already had the app on his phone. “CoMaps turned out to be really useful for me because I entered a zone with no coverage and needed it right away,” Anton recalls. A few days later, he joined as a volunteer with a rescue team that had come from El Salvador—firefighters, Civil Protection, and several different rescue units—operating remotely under instructions from a command center back home. Most of the team didn’t know CoMaps. Only one person had access to a map and was guiding the rest. Anton started helping with logistics: when the command center asked to move the team from one point to another, he was the one who could find the destination, if it was mapped at all.

Along the way, he discovered that the team’s map coordinator was using a different, much more limited navigation app, one that didn’t carry the detail that comes from a community of volunteers mapping a place, or the ability to work fully offline in a zone with no signal. When Anton showed him CoMaps, it was, in his words, “a discovery he loved making.” That’s the kind of moment that made us want to hear the fuller story. So we sat down with Anton Wenemoser and Bastian Greshake Tzovaras, co-founders of CoMaps, to talk about how the app was used during the Venezuela earthquake activation, and how a collaboration between the Humanitarian OpenStreetMap (HOT) and CoMaps came together in real time. Here’s what they told us about the app, the timing, and what they hope comes next.

CoMaps is an app built on OpenStreetMap data, map data edited collaboratively, “something like Wikipedia,” explains Anton, one of the project’s founders. Non-commercial, fully open source, and designed to work 100% offline: once you’ve downloaded the map for a region, the app keeps working even without internet or cell coverage, relying only on your phone’s GPS. By design, it’s ideal for emergencies. But for much of its first year, CoMaps had a major limitation: updating the maps took days. Processing raw OpenStreetMap data into maps ready for use required a powerful server, and every map update depended on an app update, subject to the app stores’ own review processes. That changed months before anyone needed it to.

CoMaps and HOT had already been in conversation before the earthquake. In April, Anton and Bastian, also on the CoMaps team, presented the project to HOT’s Geospatial Tech Working Group. Technical improvements had already been made, like the ability to share downloaded maps from a local device, simply because they fit the project’s vision, not because HOT had asked for them. “Many of the things we’ve already done that turned out useful to HOT are not things we sat down and thought would be useful to HOT, we were already working on them because they generally fit the vision we have for CoMaps,” Bastian explains. “We wanted people not to rely on us as the gatekeepers of the maps. And it turns out that’s also really useful for deploying maps in the field.” During the first phase of the response, volunteers mapped and updated the map on Tasking Manager, data that was then updated in CoMaps.

Between April and the earthquake, the team decoupled map updates from app updates, and with a more powerful server, processing time dropped from 10 days to about 3. The result: fresh map data released every week, for the whole planet. When Emilio Mariscal, from HOT’s team, reached out asking if they could get fresh maps of Venezuela, CoMaps was already generating one. “It was just very lucky with the timing,” Bastian says of the coincidence. But it wasn’t luck—the work by the amazing CoMaps team has built on months of prior work: the thousands of buildings mapped by volunteers through HOT’s Tasking Manager reached the screens of people responding on the ground.

That may be CoMaps’ most important contribution: it isn’t built for geospatial data specialists, it’s built for everyone. Which makes it usable by organizations that don’t have the technical capacity to download and analyze OpenStreetMap data directly. Céline Jacquin, HOT’s Latin America Senior Manager, put it this way: “We’ve had a direct dialogue so that this everyday-use app would put emergency needs more on its radar, that’s not its focus, and instead of updating its base and navigation map a few times a month, do it with total regularity, following the rhythm of emergency mapping on OSM. That allows it to be used by actors on the ground who don’t know how to download and analyze OSM data, but need to move around for their logistics. Techo, the Red Cross, and other actors can now see the mapping very quickly in a navigation app.” Bastian compares it to photography: “the best camera is the one you actually have with you, because if the camera just sits at home, you never take any pictures.” The same is true of maps. The best map app in the field is the one already in your pocket, the one you already know how to use, not the one that requires training in the exact moment there’s no time left to learn.

The CoMaps team works as volunteers, and getting fresh maps to people responding to a disaster is part of why they do this work at all. Today, when an emergency hits, the team can prioritize an update and have new maps ready to download in the field about 35 hours later. Their vision for the future goes further: region-specific updates every 12 hours, instead of one weekly update for the entire planet, so communities affected by a disaster can rely on maps that reflect what was just mapped, not what was mapped a week earlier. “I think our missions are quite aligned when it comes to what we do and why we do it,” Bastian says. For Anton, knowing CoMaps is one of the tools HOT relies on is deeply meaningful—the project exists for a humanitarian purpose, not a commercial one, and that’s its own reward: knowing it’s serving humanity. Becoming a real part of the bridge HOT builds between humanitarian response and OpenStreetMap, he says, is one of the team’s biggest goals.

Meanwhile, in Venezuela, a rescuer who once relied only on a much more limited navigation app now also has CoMaps on his phone. And in the next emergency, anywhere in the world, someone is likely to find their way again, thanks to a map built building by building, by volunteers who never met each other in person.
