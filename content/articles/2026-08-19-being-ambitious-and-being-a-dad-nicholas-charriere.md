## hard

During the YC era, I refrained from mentioning my seven-month-old daughter to anyone. Now, several years later, I find myself navigating a reality defined by two children, a dog, and an incredibly dense schedule. My children have become the most significant aspect of my existence, yet for years, my work was my entire life. Now, those two spheres are in direct competition. I suspect I am not alone in this struggle; as Paul Graham observes in 'Having Kids,' the decision to have children can indeed diminish one's productivity. While children may provide focus to some, for those whose lives are already meticulously organized, parenthood simply reduces the available time to execute that organization.

Before children, existence was characterized by a certain straightforwardness. I worked relentlessly, centering my life around my passions: computers, technology, and friends. I relocated to San Francisco, contributed to extraordinary companies, met a wonderful partner, and cultivated a robust relationship. A vast portion of my time was dedicated to professional pursuits, yet there remained an abundance of surplus time. It turns out that one possesses a tremendous amount of time when one has no dependents. I hold a profound admiration for builders, having devoured numerous biographies of great founders, yet this pursuit has led me to an uncomfortable realization: the majority of these figures are inadequate parents.

The historical record is littered with these patterns. Steve Jobs famously abandoned his daughter, Lisa, because the timing was not right for his focus on Apple. Einstein's family life was similarly fractured; he abandoned one child and was so perpetually lost in professional daydreams that his wife eventually divorced him to take their other children. From Elon Musk, who possesses so many children and ventures that significant time with any single one is mechanically impossible, to legends like Edison, Ford, and Ferrari, the pattern persists. Most biographies of iconic founders contain a bleak subtext of parental neglect.

I am familiar with many ambitious parents, and this combination is far from rare. Most of them are willing to make a choice that I find unacceptable: the delegation of parenting. I do not subscribe to the theory that 'quality time' is a sufficient substitute for presence. On the contrary, I firmly believe in maximizing the quantity of time spent with children, as there appear to be no diminishing returns on their development regarding presence. This reality is problematic. The necessity of time increases as children grow younger, which coincidentally aligns perfectly with the prime of my career. Even as a productive and organized individual, a comparison of my weekly output as a parent versus my output when I was childless reveals a visible, undeniable gap.

Everyone understands that life is short, but since becoming a parent, it feels ten times shorter. It is a frustrating reality. I am inherently ambitious, waking up every morning with a burning desire to build remarkable things. In this, I diverge from Paul Graham: I must admit that while being ambitious is a core part of my identity, parenthood might make one less ambitious. I myself am not less ambitious, but I am also ambitious about being an excellent father. My observations suggest a strict dichotomy: great fathers and great achievers rarely overlap. While exceptions exist in figures like Paul Graham, DHH, Jeff Dean, or Mark Zuckerberg, the pattern remains elusive.

Even if a perfect pattern for balancing these roles cannot be found, I refuse to accept defeat. I do not possess a magical solution, and I am certainly voicing a frustration, but I am simultaneously rejecting the false choice between these two paths. I have redefined ambition for myself: true ambition is the pursuit of being both a great father and a builder of great things. I acknowledge that this is significantly more difficult than aiming for a single objective, as each is demanding in its own right. However, difficulty is the very essence of ambition.

My strategy for navigating this complexity is simple: maintain focus, eliminate trivialities, and relentlessly improve execution. I achieve this by clarifying my professional goals, prioritizing my health to maximize energy, and establishing strict rules for family time—such as weekday dinners and prioritizing family on weekends—while completely excising time-wasting activities. Through discipline and clear objectives, I aim to achieve compounding gains. I share this because the sentiment is rarely voiced: be ambitious enough to be an ambitious dad.
